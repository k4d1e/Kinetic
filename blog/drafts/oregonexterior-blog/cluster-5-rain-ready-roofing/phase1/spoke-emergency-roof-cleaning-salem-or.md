## What to Do Right Now: Triage Steps

If you have an active roof emergency, do these four things in order:

### Step 1: Contain the Water (0–15 minutes)

Place buckets under active drips. The entry point on the roof may be 10-20 feet upslope from where you see the drip. Poke a small hole in any bulging drywall with a screwdriver and bucket underneath — this relieves hydrostatic pressure and prevents ceiling collapse.

Do not access the attic during active leaks. Wet framing is slippery. Leave source-level intervention to professionals.

### Step 2: Document Everything (15–30 minutes)

Photograph interior damage and exterior conditions from the ground — missing shingles, lifted flashing, debris on roof. Video the active leak if possible. Date and time stamp everything. Your insurance claim depends on documentation made before repair work begins.

### Step 3: Call for Emergency Service (30 minutes)

Call a roofing contractor with emergency response capability. Ask specifically:

- "What is your response time for an active leak today?"
- "Do you tarp-and-secure, or do you attempt permanent repair in emergency conditions?"
- "What is your emergency service call fee?"

Honest answers to these three questions tell you everything. A contractor who promises same-day permanent repair during a November storm event is either lying or inexperienced. Tarp-and-secure is the standard emergency protocol. Permanent repair waits for dry conditions.

### Step 4: Prevent Secondary Damage (Ongoing)

Move furniture and electronics away from wet areas. Run a dehumidifier in the affected space. If the leak is above an electrical panel, switch off the affected circuit at the breaker. Water and electricity don't negotiate.

## The Salem Factor: Why Keizer and South Salem Get Hit Different

Salem's soil profile matters for emergency repairs. The Willamette Silt and Woodburn clay series that underlie Keizer and south Salem exhibit seasonal shrink-swell behavior. During wet months, the clay expands. During dry months, it contracts. This cyclical movement shifts foundations, which shifts framing, which opens flashing joints that were sealed when installed.

A home near Cascades Gateway Park built on Woodburn clay may develop flashing failures not from roof-level causes but from foundation movement below. The emergency repair addresses the symptom. The real fix often involves both roofing services and site drainage.

The 2026 ORSC recognizes this in its foundation drainage requirements. ## Insurance: What Gets Covered and What Doesn't

Oregon homeowner's insurance typically covers:

- **Sudden storm damage**: Wind-blown shingles, fallen tree limbs, hail impact
- **Water damage from covered events**: Interior damage from a storm-caused leak

Oregon homeowner's insurance typically does **not** cover:

- **Maintenance failures**: A leak caused by 10 years of moss growth isn't "sudden"
- **Gradual deterioration**: Slow leaks that develop over months or years
- **Code upgrade costs**: If your repair triggers a code compliance upgrade, the upgrade portion is usually excluded

The distinction between "storm damage" and "deferred maintenance" is where most claim disputes live.
