# The 4-Step "Rain-Ready" Checklist for PNW Roofs

## The Rule: All Four Steps or None of Them Matter

Your roof is a system. Not a surface, not a product — a system with four interdependent subsystems that either work together or fail together.

Skip the gutter cleaning but nail the roof cleaning? Debris backs water up under your starter course. Clean the gutters but ignore the flashing? Water bypasses the gutter entirely and enters your wall cavity. Fix the flashing but never balance the ventilation? Condensation rots your decking from below while you're protecting it from above.

Every home exterior service for your PNW roof maps to one of four steps. Execute all four between August and September — before the October rains arrive — and your roof is engineered to handle whatever winter delivers. Skip one, and you've created the weak point that the other three can't compensate for.

This is the checklist. Numbered. In order. With pass/fail criteria for each step so you know exactly where you stand.

## Step 1: Roof Surface Cleaning and Assessment

**When:** August — Early September
**Duration:** 2-4 hours for a standard residential roof
**What it covers:** Moss removal, algae treatment, debris clearing, granule assessment, shingle integrity check

### The Process

A proper roof cleaning is not a pressure wash. Pressure washing destroys granule adhesion and drives water under shingle laps. The PNW standard is a soft-wash protocol: low-pressure application of a sodium percarbonate or zinc-sulfate solution that kills biological growth at the root level, followed by gentle rinse and mechanical removal of dead moss mats.

In Portland's Hillsdale neighborhood — where the mature tree canopy descending from Council Crest Park creates heavy shade on north-facing roof planes — biological growth can establish a continuous moss mat in a single season. Properties below Council Crest Park are particularly susceptible because the elevation gradient concentrates moisture runoff from the park's tree canopy onto the residential roofs below.

The assessment portion is as important as the cleaning. While the crew is on the roof, they're inspecting:

- **Granule density**: Are there bare patches where asphalt substrate is exposed? A healthy shingle has uniform granule coverage. Bare spots indicate UV degradation zones that will become leak points.
- **Shingle adhesion**: Are the thermally-activated adhesive strips still bonded between courses? In Oregon's mild climate, these strips sometimes never fully activate during installation, leaving shingles vulnerable to wind uplift from day one.
- **Fastener penetrations**: Are any nail heads visible (indicating backed-out fasteners)? Is the sealant around plumbing vents and other penetrations intact?
- **Valley condition**: Are the valley shingle courses properly woven or cut? Is the valley flashing (if open-valley design) corroded or debris-packed?

### Pass/Fail Criteria

- **PASS**: No moss mats thicker than 1/4 inch. Less than 5% of total shingle area showing bare asphalt. No lifted shingle edges. No backed-out fasteners visible. Valley flow channels clear.
- **FAIL**: Continuous moss mat on any roof plane. Granule loss exposing substrate in patches larger than 6 inches. Any lifted or curled shingles. Visible nail pops. Valley debris damming.

### If You Fail

Moss mats thicker than 1/4 inch require professional soft-wash treatment. Granule loss beyond 5% of total area means the shingle is past cleaning — it needs replacement planning. Lifted shingles and nail pops require repair-level intervention, not just cleaning.

The Portland Hills Silt that underlies Hillsdale retains moisture at the surface longer than sandy soils. This means the roof-to-ground moisture cycle is slower — your roof stays wet longer after rain, biological organisms have more growth hours, and the window between "cleanable" and "beyond cleaning" is shorter than in well-drained areas.

The 2026 ORSC doesn't mandate roof cleaning, but it does mandate that re-roofing projects address biological damage to the sheathing below. If your sheathing is soft when the shingles come off, the re-roofing cost doubles. Annual cleaning prevents sheathing degradation.

## Step 2: Gutter System Service — Clean, Align, Test

**When:** September (after the first leaf drop but before sustained rain)
**Duration:** 1-3 hours for standard residential gutters
**What it covers:** Debris removal, downspout clearing, slope verification, seam inspection, capacity test

### The Process

Gutter cleaning in the PNW isn't optional and it isn't annual. It's biannual at minimum — September and January. The September cleaning prepares for winter. The January cleaning clears the mid-season debris accumulation that inevitably builds up despite your best efforts.

The cleaning is the easy part. What separates a gutter cleaning from a gutter system service is what comes after:

**Slope verification**: Gutters should slope toward downspouts at 1/16 inch per foot. Fastener fatigue and ice loading cause sag over time, creating flat spots where water pools. A level confirms slope. Any flat or back-sloped section needs re-fastening.

**Seam inspection**: Sectional gutters have joints every 10 feet. These joints rely on sealant that fails after 5-10 years. A seam leak drips directly onto the sub-fascia — the structural board supporting the gutter and connecting to the rafter tails. Sub-fascia rot is invisible from the ground and expensive to repair.

**Downspout capacity test**: Run a garden hose at full volume into each gutter run. Water should exit the downspout within seconds. If it backs up, there's an obstruction — usually elbow joints packed with decomposed fir needles or root-blocked drain tile connections.

**End cap and outlet integrity**: Push on each end cap and outlet connector. If it flexes or shows sealant cracking, it needs resealing.

### Pass/Fail Criteria

- **PASS**: All gutters drain completely within 60 seconds of hose test. Slope is positive toward all downspouts. No seam leaks visible during flow test. All end caps firm. Downspouts discharge at least 4 feet from foundation.
- **FAIL**: Standing water in any gutter section after flow test. Back-slope detected on any run. Seam drips during flow. Loose end caps. Downspouts discharging against foundation wall or into saturated soil within 4 feet.

### If You Fail

Slope correction requires re-fastening the gutter hangers — a straightforward repair but one that requires a ladder and gutter experience. Seam failures need cleaning, priming, and re-sealing with polyurethane sealant (not silicone — silicone fails in standing-water conditions). Downspout discharge issues require extension additions or underground drain connection.

In Hillsdale, where terrain slopes toward the Fanno Creek watershed, downspout discharge direction is critical. Water draining toward the hillside below contributes to slope saturation. Portland's Title 24 stormwater overlay requires on-site runoff management. Your gutter system isn't just protecting your foundation — it's managing the neighborhood's water budget.

## Step 3: Flashing Inspection and Repair

**When:** September (concurrent with or immediately after gutter service)
**Duration:** 1-2 hours for inspection; repair time varies
**What it covers:** Step flashing, kick-out flashing, counter-flashing, valley flashing, pipe boot seals, drip edge continuity

### The Process

Flashing is where Oregon roofs fail. Not because contractors don't install it, but because they install it to "good enough" standards that don't survive PNW conditions.

The inspection checklist:

**Step flashing at sidewall transitions**: Each piece should overlap the one below by at least 2 inches, tuck behind wall cladding above, and extend under the shingle course below. If you can see the bottom edge of any step flashing piece from the ground, it's not tucked far enough.

**Kick-out flashing**: The single most important flashing detail on any Oregon roof. Where a roof slope meets a sidewall above a lower wall, water must be kicked outward into the gutter — not down behind the siding. If kick-out flashing is missing, every rain event pumps water into the wall cavity.

The 2026 ORSC now requires kick-out flashing at all roof-to-wall transitions. Millions of older Oregon homes have none. Check every junction. If a roof plane ends at a wall with a wall surface below, kick-out flashing must be present.

**Counter-flashing at chimneys**: The upper piece embedded in chimney mortar that overlaps base/step flashing below. Must be mechanically fastened into a reglet — surface-mounted sealant alone fails within 3-5 years in Oregon's freeze-thaw cycling.

**Valley flashing**: Open metal valleys should show clean metal, no debris dams. Closed-cut or woven valleys should show a smooth surface with no lifting at the cut edge.

**Pipe boot seals**: Rubber boots around plumbing vents dry-crack after 8-12 years. A cracked boot is a direct penetration. It leaks. Period.

**Drip edge continuity**: Continuous at eaves and rakes — no gaps. Missing drip edge lets water curl under the shingle edge and wick onto decking via surface tension.

### Pass/Fail Criteria

- **PASS**: All step flashing pieces overlapping and tucked. Kick-out flashing present at every roof-to-wall transition above a lower wall. Counter-flashing mechanically fastened, not just sealed. Valley flow channels clear and flat. All pipe boots flexible and crack-free. Drip edge continuous with no gaps.
- **FAIL**: Any missing kick-out flashing (automatic fail — this is the #1 leak source in Oregon). Any cracked pipe boot. Counter-flashing relying on sealant alone. Gaps in drip edge. Step flashing visible from ground level.

### If You Fail

Missing kick-out flashing is an emergency-priority repair regardless of season. Every rain event without it pumps water into your wall. Cracked pipe boots are a $50 part and 30-minute repair. Counter-flashing repointing is a half-day job. These home exterior services prevent catastrophic failure for minimal cost.

## Step 4: Ventilation Verification

**When:** September (best done during attic access for leak inspection)
**Duration:** 30-60 minutes for inspection
**What it covers:** Soffit vent airflow, ridge vent continuity, attic temperature differential, moisture assessment

### The Process

Ventilation is the invisible layer. It doesn't shed water. It doesn't direct water. It manages the vapor pressure balance between your attic space and the exterior environment.

The 2026 ORSC requires a minimum of 1:150 net free ventilation area ratio (net free area of vent openings to attic floor area). Balanced intake (soffit) and exhaust (ridge) is the target. Unbalanced ventilation — all exhaust and no intake, or vice versa — creates pressure differentials that actually pull moisture into the attic.

**Soffit vent check**: Look up at your soffits from the ground. Can you see vent openings? Are they painted over? Blocked by insulation from inside? From the attic side, you should see daylight through the soffit vents. If insulation is pressed against the sheathing with no air channel, the soffit vents are functionally dead.

Proper installation includes baffles — rigid channels that maintain an air gap between insulation and sheathing from soffit to ridge. Without baffles, blown-in insulation migrates to the eaves and seals off intake.

**Ridge vent check**: From the attic, look at the ridge. You should see a continuous 1-2 inch gap at the peak. This gap feeds the ridge vent above. If it's sealed, blocked, or non-existent, your exhaust pathway is dead.

**Temperature differential**: On a sunny September afternoon, measure attic vs. exterior temperature. A well-ventilated attic stays within 10-15°F of exterior. If yours is 30°F above, ventilation is inadequate. In winter, that differential drives condensation — warm moist air condenses on cold sheathing, creating the same damage as a roof leak from the inside out.

**Moisture assessment**: Feel the underside of the sheathing at several locations. It should be dry. Any dampness, staining, or mold indicates moisture accumulation. In the Hillsdale area, where Council Crest's elevation creates temperature inversions that trap moist air in the valley below, attic condensation is particularly aggressive November through February.

### Pass/Fail Criteria

- **PASS**: Soffit vents unobstructed and visible from ground level. Baffles installed maintaining air channel from soffit to ridge. Ridge vent continuous with no gaps or blockages. Attic temperature within 15°F of exterior on a warm day. Sheathing dry to the touch at all inspection points.
- **FAIL**: Soffit vents painted over or blocked by insulation. No baffles present. Ridge vent missing, damaged, or discontinuous. Attic temperature more than 20°F above exterior. Any moisture, staining, or mold on sheathing underside.

### If You Fail

Ventilation corrections range from simple (clearing painted-over soffit vents: $200-$400) to moderate (baffles and soffit venting: $800-$1,500) to significant (cutting a new ridge vent: $1,500-$3,000). All are a fraction of the decking replacement poor ventilation eventually causes.

Under the 2026 ORSC, any re-roofing project triggers ventilation compliance. Planning a re-roof in 2-3 years? Budget for ventilation upgrades now. It's mandatory — better to plan than be surprised on the estimate.

## The Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Doing Step 2 Without Step 1

Cleaning gutters without cleaning the roof first means you're cleaning gutters twice. The debris on your roof becomes gutter debris within two rain events. Always start from the top and work down.

### Mistake 2: Skipping Step 3 Entirely

Flashing inspection is the least-requested home exterior service and the most consequential. Homeowners clean roofs and gutters because they can see debris. Flashing failures are invisible until they produce interior damage. We find kick-out flashing deficiencies every year on homes that have been religiously cleaned. The cleaning didn't prevent the leak because it wasn't a cleaning problem.

### Mistake 3: Assuming Ventilation is "Fine"

If your home was built before 2000 and you've never had the ventilation assessed, it's probably not fine. Pre-2000 Oregon construction routinely under-ventilated attic spaces. The standard was 1:300 (half the current 1:150 requirement), and many homes didn't meet even that.

### Mistake 4: Waiting Until October

Oregon's roofing contractors book September 3-4 weeks in advance. If you call in October, you're scheduling for November. By November, you've already missed the preparation window and you're reacting instead of preventing.

Book in August. Execute in September. Relax in October.
