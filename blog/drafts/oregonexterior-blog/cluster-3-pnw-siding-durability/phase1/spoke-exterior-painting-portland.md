# 5 Warning Signs Your Oregon Siding is Hiding Structural Rot

## What You See vs. What's Happening

You notice the paint peeling on the north side of your house. Bubbles forming under the surface. Maybe a dark stain bleeding through near a window frame. You think: "Time to repaint."

Hold that thought.

In Oregon — specifically in Portland's older neighborhoods like Sellwood-Moreland — what looks like a paint problem is frequently a siding problem. And what looks like a siding problem is frequently a sheathing problem. And what looks like a sheathing problem is sometimes a framing problem.

Each layer hides the one behind it. Paint hides siding damage. Siding hides sheathing decay. Sheathing hides structural rot. By the time the symptom reaches the surface, the underlying failure has been progressing for years.

This isn't alarmism. It's physics. Portland averages 154 days of measurable precipitation per year. The heavy urban canopy — Douglas fir, big leaf maple, western red cedar — creates shade that slows drying and dumps biological material (moss, algae, leaf litter) onto every horizontal and low-slope surface. The Portland Hills Silt soils that underlie much of the west side hold moisture and contribute to elevated ground-level humidity.

Sellwood-Moreland sits in the curve between the Willamette River and the bluffs below Westmoreland. The microclimate is damp. Walk across the Sellwood Bridge on a January morning and the fog sits in that river bend like it owns the place. The homes there — many dating to the 1910s through 1940s — have been managing that moisture for over a century. Some are winning. Some aren't.

Here are the five warning signs that tell you which category your home falls into.

## Warning Sign 1: Paint Blistering in Bubbles (Not Peeling in Sheets)

### What You See

Paint lifting away from the surface in rounded bubbles or blisters, typically on north and west exposures. The blisters may contain moisture when you pop them. The surrounding paint may still be adhering firmly.

### What's Actually Happening

Paint peels in sheets when the bond between paint and substrate fails — usually from poor surface prep, applying over dirty or chalky surfaces, or incompatible coating chemistry. That's a paint problem. You fix it by scraping, priming, and repainting.

Paint blisters in bubbles when moisture vapor pushes outward through the substrate from behind. The moisture hits the paint film — which is a vapor retarder — and can't escape. Pressure builds. The film lifts. Blister.

This is vapor drive in action. Warm interior air carries moisture through the wall cavity, through the sheathing, through the siding, and hits the exterior paint. In Oregon's seven-month heating season, this process runs continuously. The paint isn't failing. The wall is telling you it has no other way to release moisture.

### The Physics

The dew point — the temperature at which water vapor condenses into liquid — often falls within the wall cavity during Oregon's heating season. When that happens, liquid water forms on the back side of the sheathing or the back face of the siding. That water is trapped. It can't dry inward (interior vapor retarder, insulation, drywall). It can't dry outward (paint film, or a siding material with low permeability). It sits. It feeds fungi.

### What to Do

Don't repaint. First, determine whether the wall has a functional drainage plane and whether the substrate behind the paint is still sound. Use a moisture meter — readings above 19% at the siding surface indicate active moisture accumulation in the wall assembly.

If the substrate is sound and moisture levels are moderate (15–19%), the fix may be improving ventilation behind the cladding. If the substrate is compromised (readings above 22%, screwdriver test fails), the "painting problem" is a siding replacement project.

## Warning Sign 2: Dark Staining at Butt Joints and Inside Corners

### What You See

Vertical dark streaks running down from the junction where two siding boards meet end-to-end (butt joints). Similar staining at inside corners where two wall planes intersect. The staining may be brown, gray, or black, and it may not wipe off — it's in the substrate, not on the surface.

### What's Actually Happening

Butt joints and inside corners are the two most vulnerable points in any siding installation. They're where capillary action concentrates moisture.

Capillary action pulls water into tight spaces. The gap between two siding boards at a butt joint — typically 1/16" to 1/8" — is the perfect capillary channel. Rain hits the wall face, runs down to the joint, and gets sucked into the gap. If the joint isn't flashed with a spline or backed by a strip of WRB material, that water reaches the sheathing.

Inside corners are worse. Two wall planes create a channel that concentrates runoff. The corner trim — typically a 1x1 or metal channel — traps moisture behind it. The sheathing at inside corners dries slower than anywhere else on the wall because air circulation is minimal.

The dark staining you see is extractive bleeding (tannins leaching from wood) or, more concerning, fungal colonization. If the staining smells musty or earthy, it's fungi. The wood behind the siding is being consumed.

### Oregon-Specific Context

The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code now requires splined or flashed butt joints on all fiber cement installations. This addresses the capillary problem at the joint itself. But the code doesn't retroactively fix the millions of existing butt joints on Portland homes that were installed without flashing.

In Sellwood-Moreland, the pre-war homes with original cedar lap siding have butt joints every 4 to 8 feet — wherever the boards were spliced. The original builders often used lead-primed cedar with no joint flashing, relying on the wood's natural resistance and the paint's impermeability to manage moisture. A hundred years later, the extractives are depleted and the paint cycles have compounded previous layers without addressing the root issue.

### What to Do

Probe the sheathing behind stained butt joints with a screwdriver or awl. If the tool sinks more than 1/8", the sheathing is compromised at that location. Mark every stained joint and test each one. If more than 20% of your butt joints show soft sheathing, you're looking at a re-siding project, not a repair.

## Warning Sign 3: Siding Boards Cupping or Warping

### What You See

Individual siding boards curving away from the wall (cupping), twisting along their length, or lifting at the bottom edge. This is most visible on lap siding — both cedar and fiber cement — when you sight down the wall plane at a low angle.

### What's Actually Happening

Wood and wood-fiber products respond to moisture differentials. When one face of a board is wetter than the other, the wet face expands. The dry face doesn't. The board cups toward the dry side.

In Oregon, the exposed face of siding dries faster than the back face (which is against the WRB or sheathing). During a rain event, the exposed face absorbs water first. Between rain events, the exposed face dries first. This constant cycling — wet/dry, wet/dry, 154 times per year — creates cumulative dimensional stress.

If the back of the siding is trapped against the sheathing with no air gap, the back face stays wet long after the front face dries. The differential is extreme. The board cups. Hard.

Cedar siding that was never back-primed (sealed on the back face before installation) is particularly vulnerable. The raw back face absorbs moisture freely. The painted front face doesn't. The resulting differential cups the board forward, opening the bottom edge and creating a new entry point for bulk water. The cycle accelerates.

### The Fastening Factor

Cupped boards also tell you about fastening. A properly face-nailed cedar lap board — two nails per stud, driven through the overlap into the board below — resists cupping by mechanical constraint. A single-nailed board, or a board nailed too high (above the overlap line), has no constraint at the bottom edge. It curls freely.

James Hardie fiber cement can cup if installed without the manufacturer's specified gap at butt joints. Without the 1/8" relief gap, the board has no room to move. It deforms instead.

### What to Do

Isolated cupped boards can be removed, back-primed, and reinstalled with proper fastening. Widespread cupping across a wall face indicates systemic moisture imbalance — the wall assembly lacks a drying mechanism. The siding itself may be salvageable, but the system behind it needs to be redesigned with a drainage plane and back-ventilation gap.

## Warning Sign 4: Soft Spots at the Foundation Line

### What You See

The bottom course of siding — the boards closest to the ground — feels soft when pressed. The paint may be intact. The color may look fine. But when you push with your thumb, there's give. When you probe with a screwdriver, it sinks.

### What's Actually Happening

The bottom course takes the most punishment on any wall. It's closest to splash-back from rain hitting the ground. It's closest to soil moisture wicking upward through the foundation. It's closest to ground-level vegetation and organic debris that hold moisture against the wall.

Portland's soils — the Portland Hills Silt and Missoula Flood deposits that underlie most of the metro area — are moisture-retentive. Homes in Sellwood-Moreland, near the Willamette River floodplain, sit on particularly wet soil. The ambient ground moisture migrates into the foundation, through the mudsill, and into the bottom plate of the wall. The bottom course of siding is the exit point.

The 2026 ORSC requires a minimum 6" clearance between siding and finished grade. Many older Portland homes have 2" or less. Some have siding in direct soil contact. At that point, the siding isn't managing moisture — it's absorbing it continuously.

### The Hidden Damage

Soft bottom-course siding almost always means the mudsill or bottom plate behind it is also compromised. The sheathing in the bottom 12–18" of the wall is likely decayed. This is no longer a siding problem. It's a structural problem.

In earthquake-prone Oregon — the Cascadia Subduction Zone generates the state's seismic design requirements — a compromised mudsill means the wall is no longer properly tied to the foundation. The hold-down connections, anchor bolts, and shear transfer mechanisms all depend on sound wood at the base of the wall.

### What to Do

This is not a DIY assessment. If you have soft siding at the foundation line, get a professional inspection that includes removing a section of siding and sheathing to evaluate the mudsill and bottom plate. If structural members are compromised, a licensed contractor needs to design a repair that maintains the structural integrity of the wall-to-foundation connection.

## Warning Sign 5: Moss or Algae Growth Returning Within Months of Cleaning

### What You See

You pressure-wash the siding. It looks great. Six weeks later, green is creeping back. By three months, it looks like you never cleaned it. On north and west exposures, the moss is thicker than it was before you cleaned.

### What's Actually Happening

Moss and algae don't grow on dry surfaces. If biological growth returns within weeks of cleaning, the surface is staying wet long enough to support colonization. The cleaning removed the symptom. The moisture condition that allows growth is unchanged.

Portland's biological load is extreme. Douglas fir and western red cedar canopy dumps organic debris — needles, pollen, bark particles — onto roof and wall surfaces year-round. Big leaf maple contributes leaf matter and surface texture that retains moisture. The combination creates a substrate for biological growth that few other U.S. cities match.

Rapid re-colonization tells you one or more of these conditions exists:

1. **No back-ventilation:** The siding retains moisture on its back face. The surface never fully dries between rain events. Biological organisms exploit the sustained moisture.
2. **Heavy canopy shade:** The wall receives less than 2 hours of direct sunlight per day. Solar UV and heat are the primary biological controls. Without them, anything organic on the surface will be colonized.
3. **Failing paint or sealant:** The protective coating is no longer repelling moisture at the surface level. The siding material itself is absorbing rain and providing a hospitable environment.

Portland's Tree Code (Title 11.50) limits how much canopy you can remove, even on your own property. In Sellwood-Moreland's mature tree neighborhoods, you may not be able to solve the shade problem by cutting trees. You have to solve it at the wall assembly level — materials and details that manage moisture without relying on solar drying.

### What to Do

Don't pressure-wash again. You're removing the protective patina and driving water into the substrate. Instead:

1. Treat with a sodium percarbonate cleaner (oxygen bleach) — low pressure, high dwell time.
2. Install zinc or copper strips at the top of affected wall sections. Rain washes metallic ions down the wall face, inhibiting biological growth.
3. Evaluate the wall assembly for drainage and ventilation. If the siding is retaining moisture from behind, no surface treatment will solve the biological growth problem long-term.

## The Common Thread

All five warning signs point to the same root cause: moisture that enters the wall assembly faster than it can leave. The symptoms are different — paint blisters, staining, cupping, soft spots, biological growth — but the physics is identical.

Oregon's climate creates a moisture load that exceeds the drying capacity of most wall assemblies built before 2010. The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code addresses this for new construction with mandatory drainage planes, continuous insulation, and prescriptive flashing details. But code doesn't retrofit itself. Existing homes need evaluation, diagnosis, and — where warranted — intervention.

If you've spotted one of these signs, investigate before you decorate. A screwdriver, a $30 moisture meter, and 20 minutes of probing will tell you whether you need a painter or a siding contractor.

If you've spotted three or more, you need someone to open the wall.
