# James Hardie vs. Vinyl Siding: What Actually Survives a Portland Winter?

## The Question Everyone Asks Wrong

"Should I get James Hardie or vinyl?"

That's the question we hear most on initial consultations. And it's the wrong question. Not because the answer doesn't matter — it does. But because the siding material is the outermost layer of a system with five or six layers behind it. Choosing the cladding before designing the wall assembly is like choosing tires before deciding whether you're buying a truck or a sedan.

The right question is: "Given my wall assembly, my exposure, and my budget, which cladding performs better over 30 years in Portland's climate?"

That's the question we're going to answer.

Portland sits on Portland Hills Silt over Missoula Flood deposits. That geology means high ambient moisture from the ground up. Add 154 days of rain per year, heavy urban canopy from Douglas fir and big leaf maple driving biological load, and you're asking your siding to perform in one of the most demanding moisture environments in the lower 48.

## Head-to-Head: The Data

### Material Properties

| Property | James Hardie HZ10 | Vinyl (0.044" premium) |
|---|---|---|
| **Water absorption** | 15–20% by weight | 0% |
| **Thermal expansion** | Low (cement-based) | High (~0.5" per 12' panel) |
| **Impact resistance** | Moderate (can crack) | High (flexes, returns) |
| **Fire rating** | Non-combustible | Melts at ~750°F |
| **Biological resistance** | High | High |
| **UV stability** | ColorPlus finish: 15-yr warranty | Moderate (fading over 15+ yrs) |
| **Weight** | 2.3 lb/sq ft | 0.4 lb/sq ft |
| **Vapor permeability** | Semi-permeable (allows drying) | Vapor barrier |

That last row is the one most people miss. And it changes the entire calculation for Oregon.

### The Vapor Permeability Problem

James Hardie fiber cement is semi-permeable. Moisture vapor can pass through it — slowly. This means a Hardie-clad wall can dry to the exterior. In Oregon's heating season (October through April), vapor drive pushes interior moisture outward. A semi-permeable cladding allows some of that moisture to escape through the siding face.

Vinyl is a vapor barrier. Nothing passes through it. This means all drying must occur either inward (through the interior finish and drywall — slow and limited) or through the drainage plane gap behind the vinyl.

Neither approach is inherently better. But they require different wall assemblies.

**Hardie wall design:** WRB with moderate vapor permeability → drainage gap (3/16" min) → fiber cement cladding. The wall dries both through the drainage gap (primary) and through the cladding face (secondary).

**Vinyl wall design:** WRB with moderate vapor permeability → drainage gap (3/16" min, ideally 3/8"+) → vinyl cladding with built-in ventilation slots. The wall dries exclusively through the drainage gap. That gap needs to work harder because there's no secondary drying path.

Up in the Crestwood neighborhood — elevation 1,000+ feet near Council Crest Park — homes get more wind exposure and faster drying than homes in the river valleys. A vinyl system that performs marginally in low-lying Foster-Powell might work fine at Crestwood's elevation because the wind-driven air exchange behind the cladding is more aggressive.

### Installation Complexity

**James Hardie** demands precision. The HZ10 product (specifically formulated for Climate Zone 4C — HZ stands for "HardieZone") requires:

- Blind nailing: nails placed in the top edge, covered by the next course. Face nailing voids the warranty in many configurations.
- 1-1/4" minimum penetration into framing members.
- 1/8" gap at all butt joints, caulked with Hardie-approved sealant.
- Back-clearance from any horizontal surface (roofs, decks, grade) per manufacturer specs.
- All cut edges treated with Hardie touch-up paint to maintain the warranty.

Sub-fascia integration is critical. Where siding meets the roofline, the transition from cladding to soffit requires step flashing, a kick-out diverter, and proper lapping of the WRB under the flashing. Miss the kick-out, and bulk water flows behind the siding at the roof-to-wall intersection. This is the single most common failure point we see on Hardie installations in Portland.

**Vinyl** is more forgiving. It floats on the wall — nails are driven into the center of the nailing slot, left 1/32" proud. The material expands and contracts freely. Locking mechanisms between courses create a rainscreen effect. Utility trim channels at windows, doors, and terminations allow thermal movement without buckling.

But "forgiving" isn't "foolproof." Vinyl's most common installation failure in Oregon: tight nailing. A single over-driven nail restricts thermal movement across the entire panel. Summer heat buckles the panel. The homeowner blames the material. The material isn't the problem.

### The 2026 Code Factor

The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code doesn't prefer one cladding over another. But it sets performance requirements that affect both materials differently:

**Continuous insulation:** Both Hardie and vinyl can be installed over continuous rigid foam insulation. Hardie requires long fasteners or furring strips over the foam to hit framing. Vinyl can attach to furring strips or, in some approved assemblies, directly through foam into framing with approved screws. The furring strip approach is superior for both materials because it creates the drainage gap automatically.

**Drainage provisions:** Both materials must have a drainage mechanism behind them. For Hardie, this can be a drainable WRB (crinkle-type housewrap) or a drainage mat. For vinyl, the built-in ventilation design of most premium vinyl products can satisfy drainage requirements in some jurisdictions — but check with your local building official. Portland's inspection standards are tighter than Salem's.

**Flashing integration:** Identical requirements for both materials. Head flashing at windows and doors. Pan flashing at window sills. Step flashing at roof-to-wall intersections. Kick-out diverters at roof terminations. The 2026 code made these prescriptive rather than performance-based, which means there's one right way to do it now.

These numbers assume existing sheathing is sound. If you're opening walls and finding rot, add $3–$8/sq ft for sheathing replacement, WRB, and drainage plane installation regardless of cladding choice.

Labor costs for Hardie are higher because the material is heavier (2.3 lb/sq ft vs. 0.4 lb/sq ft), requires more precise cutting (fiber cement dust requires HEPA-filtered saws per OSHA silica rules), and has a tighter fastening specification.

## The Recommendation Matrix

**Choose James Hardie when:**
- Fire resistance matters (WUI zones, close lot-line setbacks)
- You want a 30+ year paint cycle with ColorPlus factory finish
- Your wall assembly allows outward drying through the cladding
- You're doing a full wall retrofit and building the assembly from scratch
- Resale value is a priority (Hardie carries a premium in Portland's market)

**Choose vinyl when:**
- Budget is the primary constraint
- The existing wall assembly has limited drying capacity and you need zero water absorption at the cladding layer
- You're siding over existing materials as a rain-management upgrade
- The home is in a high-wind zone where impact resistance matters (vinyl flexes; Hardie cracks)
- You want low lifetime maintenance (no painting, no caulking of butt joints)

**Choose neither (choose cedar) when:**
- You own a pre-1940 home in a historic district and want to maintain architectural character
- You're prepared to commit to back-priming, full rainscreen installation, and re-staining every 3–5 years
- The aesthetic matters more than the maintenance math

There is no universally "best" siding. There's only the best siding for your specific wall assembly, exposure, and maintenance tolerance.
