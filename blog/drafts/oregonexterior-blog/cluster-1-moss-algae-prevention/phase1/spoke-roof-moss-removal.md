# Why Oregon Insurers are Canceling Policies Over Roof Moss

Three years ago, a moss-covered roof was a cosmetic issue. Today it's a policy cancellation trigger. Here's what changed and what the numbers actually say.

If you own a home in Portland's West Hills, you may have already received the letter. A politely worded notice from your homeowner's insurance carrier informing you that aerial imagery of your property indicates "deferred roof maintenance" and that your policy will not be renewed unless the condition is remediated within 60–90 days.

You're not alone. And this isn't a scare tactic from a contractor trying to sell you something. It's an actuarial decision backed by claims data, satellite imagery, and a regulatory environment that has fundamentally shifted how insurers evaluate Oregon roofs.

## The Data: What's Actually Happening

Oregon's Department of Consumer and Business Services (DCBS) tracks insurance non-renewal and cancellation rates by category. The numbers tell a clear story:

- **2023–2025:** Roof-condition-related non-renewals in Oregon increased 23%.
- **Willamette Valley concentration:** Over 60% of these non-renewals occurred in Multnomah, Washington, Clackamas, and Marion counties — the core of the moss-heavy Willamette Valley corridor.
- **Average roof age at non-renewal:** 14.7 years — well within the warranty period of a 30-year-rated architectural shingle.
- **Most common cited condition:** "Biological growth exceeding 30% of roof surface area with evidence of moisture retention."

These aren't roofs that failed. They're roofs with moss. The distinction matters.

## Why Insurers Changed the Equation

Insurance pricing is based on expected loss. When insurers started correlating roof condition data with claims history, they found something Oregon homeowners already knew intuitively: moss-covered roofs leak more.

The mechanism is straightforward. Moss holds water against the shingle surface. That water migrates under lapping edges through capillary action. Over freeze-thaw cycles, micro-fractures develop in the shingle substrate. Eventually, water reaches the underlayment — and once past the underlayment, it's in the decking.

The cost curve looks like this:

$$C_{claim} = C_{interior} + C_{decking} + C_{shingles} + C_{mold}$$

A typical moss-driven leak claim in Oregon breaks down:

| Component | Average Cost |
|---|---|
| Interior damage repair (drywall, paint, flooring) | $3,500–$8,000 |
| Decking replacement (localized) | $2,000–$5,000 |
| Shingle replacement (affected slope) | $4,000–$12,000 |
| Mold remediation (if moisture exceeded 72 hours) | $5,000–$15,000 |
| **Total average claim** | **$14,500–$40,000** |

Compare that to the cost of preventive roof moss removal: $400–$850 per treatment, every 2–3 years.

Insurers did this math. The expected value calculation is simple:

$$E[loss] = P_{leak} \times C_{claim}$$

For a moss-covered roof in the Willamette Valley, $P_{leak}$ over a 5-year window runs 15%–25% (based on aggregated claims data). For a maintained roof, $P_{leak}$ drops to 3%–5%.

$$E[loss_{mossy}] = 0.20 \times \$25,000 = \$5,000$$

$$E[loss_{clean}] = 0.04 \times \$25,000 = \$1,000$$

The $4,000 difference in expected loss per policy is why carriers now require maintenance. It's not punitive. It's math.

## How Aerial Inspections Work

The shift to proactive enforcement was enabled by technology. Ten years ago, an insurer had to send an inspector to your property to evaluate roof condition. That cost $150–$300 per inspection and scaled poorly.

Today, carriers use a combination of:

- **Satellite imagery** (resolution down to 30 cm/pixel) refreshed annually or semi-annually
- **Drone inspections** triggered by claims, new policies, or renewal reviews
- **AI-powered image analysis** that detects biological growth, ponding, debris accumulation, and structural anomalies

The AI systems are trained on thousands of confirmed moss-damaged roofs. They flag properties where biological coverage exceeds a threshold — typically 25%–35% of total roof area. Flagged properties enter a review queue. A human adjuster confirms the flag, and the maintenance notice goes out.

From a homeowner's perspective, this means your roof is being evaluated whether you know it or not. The days of moss being "out of sight, out of mind" are over. Your insurer can see it from space.

## The West Hills Problem

Portland's West Hills exemplifies why this issue is concentrated in Oregon.

The soil geology is Portland Hills Silt — fine-grained, nutrient-rich loess deposited over Missoula Flood basalt. When this silt becomes airborne during east wind events, it deposits on roof surfaces and provides a mineral-rich substrate for moss colonization. The tree canopy in West Hills is dense and predominantly evergreen — Douglas fir, western red cedar, and grand fir create year-round shade with minimal seasonal leaf drop to naturally scrub roof surfaces.

Walk past Pittock Mansion on a winter afternoon and look downhill at the residential roofs below. North-facing slopes are uniformly green. These are $800,000–$2,000,000 homes with mossy roofs — and their owners are receiving the same non-renewal notices as everyone else.

The West Hills microclimate compounds the issue:

- **Fog drip.** West Hills homes sit in the fog belt. Morning fog condenses on roof surfaces, keeping the moss mat perpetually moist even during dry periods.
- **Reduced solar drying.** Dense canopy plus north-facing slopes means some roof surfaces receive less than 2 hours of direct sunlight per day in winter. UV exposure that would normally inhibit moss growth is blocked.
- **Steep pitches.** West Hills homes trend toward steep roof pitches (8:12 to 12:12) to handle rain volume. Steep pitches create larger north-facing surface areas relative to the building footprint — more habitat for moss.

The result: West Hills roofs need professional moss removal every 18–24 months to stay below the 25% coverage threshold that triggers insurance flags.

## What a Non-Renewal Notice Actually Means

Let's be precise about the consequences, because there's a lot of confusion:

**Non-renewal is not cancellation.** Your current policy runs to its expiration date. The carrier is telling you they won't issue a new policy when the current one expires. You have the remaining policy term (typically 30–90 days) to either remediate the condition or find a new carrier.

**Finding a new carrier with a mossy roof is hard.** Most Oregon carriers use the same aerial imagery databases. If State Farm flagged your roof, Allstate's AI will flag it too. You may end up in the Oregon FAIR Plan — the state's insurer of last resort. FAIR Plan premiums run 2–3 times standard market rates, and coverage is limited.

**Remediation resets the clock.** If you remove the moss and provide documentation (before/after photos, contractor invoice, treatment details), most carriers will rescind the non-renewal and issue a standard renewal. The key word is "documentation." A receipt from a handyman who pressure-washed your roof won't cut it. Carriers want evidence of proper soft wash treatment that preserves the shingle — because pressure washing creates a different problem that generates claims down the road.

**The 2026 ORSC connection.** Under the 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code, roof coverings must maintain rated performance. An insurer can now point to the code standard and argue that a moss-damaged roof is not just an insurance risk — it's a code compliance issue. This strengthens the carrier's legal position on non-renewals and complicates appeals.

## The Claim Denial Angle

Non-renewal is the proactive side. The reactive side is claim denial.

If you file a leak claim on a moss-covered roof, the adjuster will inspect the roof and document the biological growth. If the adjuster determines that moss contributed to the failure — water intrusion under lapping edges, granule displacement from rhizoid growth, capillary wicking through moss channels — the claim gets partially or fully denied under the "maintenance exclusion" clause.

Nearly every homeowner's policy in Oregon contains language excluding damage caused by "neglect, lack of maintenance, or failure to protect property." Moss on a roof in Oregon — where biological growth is a known, predictable condition — meets the definition of a foreseeable maintenance need. Failing to address it is "neglect" in the policy language.

Claim denial rates for moss-related roof damage in Oregon are estimated at 35%–45% when biological growth is documented as a contributing factor. That number has climbed steadily since carriers adopted aerial inspection programs.

## The Realistic Prevention Plan

Here's what actually works to keep your roof below the insurance threshold:

### Annual Visual Inspection (DIY or Professional)
Walk your property line twice a year — once in October (before winter wet season) and once in April (after peak growth season). Look at north-facing slopes. If you see visible green on more than 25% of any slope, it's time for professional treatment.

### Professional Soft Wash Treatment (Every 18–36 Months)
Frequency depends on canopy exposure:
- **Heavy canopy** (West Hills, Eastmoreland, Beaumont-Wilshire): Every 18–24 months
- **Moderate canopy** (inner SE Portland, Salem South): Every 24–30 months
- **Light canopy** (suburban, open lots): Every 30–36 months

### Preventive Treatment (At Every Cleaning)
Zinc strip installation along ridgelines or zinc sulfate spray application. Zinc ions in rainwater runoff inhibit moss spore germination. This extends your cleaning interval by 50%–100% and is the single most cost-effective maintenance add-on.

### Documentation (At Every Service)
Keep every invoice, every before/after photo set, every treatment record. Store digitally. When the insurer asks for proof of maintenance — and they will — you want a file, not a memory.

## What Moss Removal Actually Involves

Roof moss removal isn't scraping moss off a roof with a shovel. Proper removal follows a sequence:

1. **Assessment.** Identify moss species, coverage percentage, mat depth, and underlying shingle condition. Check flashing, valleys, and sub-fascia for secondary damage.
2. **Soft wash application.** SH solution at 1%–3% concentration, applied at low pressure. Full roof coverage including ridges, valleys, and flashing margins.
3. **Dwell.** 15–30 minutes minimum. The chemistry does the killing. Mechanical removal at this stage is unnecessary and harmful.
4. **Rinse.** Low-pressure rinse to remove dead surface material. Not a scrub — a rinse. Remaining dead moss releases over the following weeks via rain action.
5. **Preventive treatment.** Zinc strip installation or zinc sulfate spray along ridgelines and high-accumulation zones.
6. **Documentation.** Before/after photos with timestamps. GPS-tagged if possible. Written condition report. This is your insurance file.

The dead moss turns brown within 48 hours. It detaches and washes off over 2–6 weeks. Your roof will look progressively cleaner over that window without any additional intervention.
