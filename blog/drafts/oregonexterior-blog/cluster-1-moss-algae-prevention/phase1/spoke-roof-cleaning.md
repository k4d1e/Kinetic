# Soft Wash vs. Pressure Washing: Why Oregon Homeowners are Switching

Two methods. One roof. The wrong choice costs you the warranty and possibly the roof itself.

Every spring in Portland's Beaumont-Wilshire neighborhood, you'll hear the same sound echoing off the bungalows: the high-pitched whine of a gas-powered pressure washer pointed at a roof. It's the sound of someone accelerating shingle failure at 2,800 PSI while trying to fix it.

This isn't opinion. It's material science. And the data is clear enough that every major shingle manufacturer has taken an explicit position on it. Let's lay it out side by side.

## The Two Methods, Defined

**Pressure washing** uses mechanical force — water at 2,000–3,500 PSI driven through a narrow-angle nozzle (15°–25°) — to blast moss, algae, and debris off the roof surface. The force does the work. No chemistry required.

**Soft washing** uses chemistry — a cleaning solution (typically sodium hypochlorite at 1%–3% concentration, sometimes blended with surfactants) applied at low pressure (60–100 PSI) through a wide-angle nozzle or downstream injector. The solution kills the organism. Gravity and rain do the removal over 2–6 weeks.

Both remove moss. Only one leaves the shingle intact.

## The Physics: Why Pressure Kills Composite Shingles

A standard architectural shingle (the most common type on Portland roofs) is built in layers: fiberglass mat, asphalt coating, ceramic-coite granules. The granules are the armor. They block UV, shed water, and provide fire resistance. Without them, the asphalt layer is exposed to direct sunlight and oxidizes within 18–24 months.

Here's the problem. The bond between granules and asphalt is designed to resist weathering forces — rain impact, hail, wind shear. Those forces top out around 50–80 PSI of localized pressure. A pressure washer at 2,500 PSI delivers roughly 30–50 times the force the granule bond was engineered to handle.

The result is predictable:

$$F_{washer} \gg F_{bond} \Rightarrow \text{granule displacement}$$

One pass of a pressure washer nozzle at standard operating distance (12–18 inches) strips granules in a visible swath. The shingle looks "clean" because the moss is gone. It also looks lighter — because the granule color is gone too. That lighter area is exposed asphalt with a dramatically shortened service life.

Walk through Beaumont-Wilshire or neighboring Alameda in summer. You can spot pressure-washed roofs from the street. They have an uneven, mottled appearance — clean patches with visible granule loss next to untouched areas. It's a signature.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Factor | Pressure Wash | Soft Wash |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating pressure** | 2,000–3,500 PSI | 60–100 PSI |
| **Moss removal speed** | Immediate (same day) | Gradual (2–6 weeks) |
| **Granule loss** | Significant — visible after single treatment | Negligible — no mechanical contact |
| **Shingle warranty compliance** | Voids warranty (all major manufacturers) | Compliant (CertainTeed, GAF, Owens Corning) |

The price difference on day one favors pressure washing. The 5-year cost doesn't. And the warranty math doesn't even come close.

## What the Shingle Manufacturers Say

This isn't ambiguous. Here's the language:

- **GAF** (Pro Starter/Timberline series): "Only low-pressure washing (not to exceed 100 PSI) is recommended... Use of high-pressure washing equipment may damage shingles and void the warranty."
- **CertainTeed** (Landmark series): "High-pressure washing methods are not covered maintenance procedures and may result in claim denial."
- **Owens Corning** (Duration series): Specifies "gentle cleaning methods" and explicitly excludes mechanical force removal.

When a warranty claim gets denied, the manufacturer's inspection report typically cites "evidence of improper maintenance" and includes close-up photos of granule displacement patterns consistent with pressure washing. These patterns are distinct — linear swaths of granule loss following the nozzle path, versus the random, distributed pattern of natural weathering.

## The Beaumont-Wilshire Test Case

Alberta Park sits in the center of Beaumont-Wilshire, surrounded by blocks of 1920s–1940s bungalows with moderate-to-steep pitches (6:12 to 8:12). The tree canopy here is mixed — big leaf maple, ornamental cherry, some Douglas fir on the larger lots. Moss pressure is moderate-to-heavy depending on orientation and shading.

We've serviced roofs within a four-block radius of Alberta Park where one side of the street was historically pressure-washed (by a previous service provider) and the other side was soft-washed. The difference after 5 years is measurable:

- **Pressure-washed roofs:** Average remaining granule coverage of 40%–55% on north-facing slopes. Visible asphalt exposure. One roof required full north-slope re-shingle at year 8 of a 30-year-rated shingle.
- **Soft-washed roofs:** Average remaining granule coverage of 75%–85% on north-facing slopes. No premature re-shingling required.

The shingles were the same product, installed within 2 years of each other, on houses with comparable canopy exposure. The only variable was cleaning method.

## The 2026 ORSC Angle

The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code requires roof coverings to maintain their rated performance characteristics. A shingle that's lost 50% of its granules to pressure washing isn't performing to its fire rating, impact rating, or weathering rating — regardless of age.

This creates a quiet liability. A pressure-washed roof can technically be out of code compliance even though it "looks clean." During a property sale, a home inspector who knows what granule loss looks like will flag it. That flag goes in the inspection report. The buyer's insurance carrier sees it. And now you're negotiating a roof replacement credit on a roof that's only 8 years old.

Portland's stormwater overlay (Title 24) adds a separate concern. Pressure washing sends granule-laden runoff into the stormwater system. Those granules are classified as construction sediment. Soft wash runoff contains dilute sodium hypochlorite, which requires containment and neutralization — but a legitimate operator handles this as standard procedure. Both methods have stormwater implications. Only one of them also destroys the roof.

## Soft Wash: The Chemistry Details

For transparency, here's what's in a standard soft wash solution:

- **Sodium hypochlorite (SH):** 1%–3% concentration at the shingle surface. This is household bleach chemistry, just applied precisely. SH kills moss, algae, lichen, and mildew on contact. It denatures proteins and disrupts cell membranes.
- **Surfactant:** Reduces surface tension so the SH penetrates moss mats evenly instead of beading off. Common surfactants include sodium lauryl sulfate or proprietary blends.
- **Dwell time:** 15–30 minutes depending on moss depth and temperature. Below 40°F, SH efficacy drops and dwell times need to increase.

After treatment, the moss dies in place. Over the next 2–6 weeks, rain and wind remove the dead material. The roof won't look "clean" on day one — and that's the point. Trying to make it look perfect immediately is what leads operators to over-concentrate the solution (which can damage vegetation below) or add mechanical agitation (which defeats the purpose).

## The Verdict

Pressure washing is faster, cheaper on day one, and immediately satisfying. It also strips your shingles, voids your warranty, shortens your roof life by a decade, and costs more over any time horizon longer than 18 months.

Soft washing is slower to show results and costs more upfront. It also preserves your granule bed, maintains warranty compliance, extends your maintenance interval when paired with preventive treatment, and costs less over the life of the roof.

This isn't a close call. The physics, the manufacturer specs, and the long-term economics all point the same direction.

If you've been pressure washing your roof, stop. The damage already done isn't reversible — those granules aren't coming back. But you can prevent further loss by switching to soft wash for all future maintenance and adding zinc or copper preventive treatment to extend intervals.
