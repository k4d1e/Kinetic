## The Physics of Deferred Maintenance in Oregon

Oregon doesn't destroy homes the way hurricanes do. It does it slowly. Capillary action pulls moisture through aging caulk joints at roughly 0.3 mm per hour. Over an eight-month wet season — October through May — that's enough to saturate sheathing, feed fungal colonies behind your siding, and compromise the structural integrity of sub-fascia connections you'll never see until they fail.

Portland sits on Portland Hills Silt over Missoula Flood deposits. The east hills rest on Boring Lava basalt. That geology means water doesn't drain evenly across the metro. In neighborhoods like Richmond, where the grade shifts toward the Willamette floodplain near Oaks Bottom Wildlife Refuge, hydrostatic pressure against foundations runs 15–20% higher than on the west side benches. The soil holds water. Your house sits in it.

Add the biological load — Douglas fir, big leaf maple, western red cedar — and you've got year-round moss and algae pressure on every horizontal and north-facing surface. This isn't cosmetic. Moss root systems (rhizoids) penetrate composition shingle granules within 18 months of colonization. Once embedded, they create micro-channels that accelerate water infiltration by up to 40%.

The cost of ignoring this physics is compounding. Not linearly. Exponentially.
