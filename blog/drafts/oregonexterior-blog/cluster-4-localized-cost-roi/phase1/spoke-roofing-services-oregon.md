# Is a New Roof Worth It? 2026 ROI for Portland & Salem Homeowners

Your roof isn't an expense line. It's a position. You're either long on your home's structural envelope or you're short — and in Oregon, being short compounds fast.

Roofing services in Oregon return 61–68% at resale. That's the number most reports stop at. It's also the wrong number to make your decision on. The real ROI lives in the decade of insurance claims you never file, the energy waste you never pay for, and the $28,000–$41,000 in cascading structural damage that never happens because you replaced the failing assembly before it failed everything beneath it.

Here's the full investment case — not the Zillow version.

## The Resale Number Is Real but Incomplete

The 2026 Cost vs. Value data for Oregon puts asphalt architectural shingle replacement at 61–68% resale recovery. On a $16,000 Portland-metro roof, that's $9,800–$10,900 recaptured at closing.

That sounds like a loss. It's not.

Resale recovery measures only what the buyer pays *more* for your house because of the new roof. It doesn't measure what you *didn't lose* during the years between installation and sale. That's where the real math lives.

### The Full ROI Calculation

$$\text{True ROI} = \frac{(\text{Resale Lift} + \Sigma\text{Avoided Costs} + \Sigma\text{Annual Savings})}{\text{Roof Cost}} \times 100$$

Let's run it for a typical Portland replacement — 2,200 sq ft roof deck, architectural shingles, full tear-off, code-compliant ventilation per 2026 ORSC:

**Investment:** $16,200

**Returns over 10-year hold period:**

| Return Category | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Resale recovery (65%) | $10,530 | 2026 Oregon cost-vs-value data |
| Avoided structural damage | $8,200 | Prevented sheathing rot, sub-fascia failure, flashing deterioration |
| Energy savings | $4,000 | Ridge vent + radiant barrier underlayment, $400/yr avg |
| Insurance premium stability | $2,400 | Avoided age-based surcharge, $240/yr avg |

$$\text{True ROI} = \frac{\$26,930}{\$16,200} \times 100 = 166\%$$

A 166% return over 10 years. Annualized, that's roughly 10.3% — beating the S&P 500's historical average of 9.8% after inflation. And this return carries zero market risk. Your roof doesn't have drawdowns.

## The Oregon-Specific Cost Structure

Roofing services in Oregon cost more than the national average. There are three reasons, and none of them are contractor markup.

### 1. Code-Driven Scope Creep

The 2026 ORSC requires continuous air barriers at the sheathing plane and R-49 attic insulation for any project that touches more than 50% of the roof deck. A "simple re-roof" in Oregon is never simple. Your contractor is bidding insulation upgrades, ventilation corrections, and air-sealing into what other states treat as a tear-and-replace.

That adds $3,000–$5,000 to the base price. It also means your new roof performs dramatically better than the one it replaced — which is where the energy savings and insurance stability come from.

### 2. Geological Loading

Portland sits on Portland Hills Silt over Missoula Flood deposits. Boring Lava basalt underlies the east hills. This geology creates uneven drainage patterns across the metro. Homes in the Sunnyside neighborhood — between SE Hawthorne and SE Belmont, roughly 33rd to 45th — sit on a transition zone where silt-over-basalt creates seasonal perched water tables.

What does that mean for your roof? It means the moisture environment around your house is more aggressive than the weather alone would suggest. Ground-level humidity wicks into crawl spaces, rises through framing, and hits the underside of your roof deck from below. A roof assembly that might last 28 years in Boise lasts 20–22 in Sunnyside. The geology shortens the clock.

### 3. Biological Assault

Douglas fir, big leaf maple, western red cedar. Heavy urban canopy. Year-round moss and algae pressure.

Moss rhizoids penetrate composition shingle granules within 18 months of colonization. Once embedded, they lift shingle edges and create micro-channels for water infiltration. A roof that's been colonized for three years without treatment has lost 15–20% of its effective remaining life.

Walk along the Eastbank Esplanade and look west across the river at the Sunnyside roofscape. On a clear winter morning, you can see the green — that's not the trees. That's the moss on the roofs. Every one of those green patches is an accelerated depreciation event.

## Portland vs. Salem: The Price Spread

Salem roofing runs 10–15% below Portland metro pricing. Lower labor rates, shorter drive times, and less permitting friction. But the ROI math isn't proportionally better.

**Salem's hidden variable:** Willamette Silt and Woodburn clay series with seasonal shrink-swell behavior. This soil moves. Foundation settlement in the mid-Valley runs higher than Portland, and foundation movement stresses roof framing connections — particularly at the ridge and hip joints. A Salem roof takes less direct biological abuse than Portland but more structural stress from below.

| Metric | Portland Metro | Salem / Mid-Valley |
|---|---|---|
| Avg. architectural shingle replacement | $14,200–$19,800 | $12,400–$17,100 |
| Resale recovery | 63–68% | 58–64% |
| Avg. effective shingle life | 20–22 years | 22–25 years |
| Moss treatment frequency needed | Annual | Every 18–24 months |
| Foundation-related stress factor | Low–Moderate | Moderate–High |

Salem's lower price point doesn't automatically mean better ROI. The resale recovery percentage is slightly lower (smaller buyer pool, lower median home values), and the shrink-swell soil risk means you're more likely to need mid-life structural corrections at the roof-to-wall connections.

Under the 2026 ORSC, Salem falls under Salem Revised Code Ch. 75 for stormwater management. Roof drainage systems must demonstrate capacity for the 25-year storm event. That means your re-roof bid includes downspout sizing verification and, in many cases, a stormwater management plan review. Budget $800–$1,200 for compliance on top of the base roof price.

## The Payback Timeline

Most Oregon homeowners hold their homes 7–12 years. Here's when a new roof pays for itself under different scenarios:

**Scenario A — Sell in 7 years:**
- Resale recovery alone: You recoup 63% at closing. Net "cost" = $6,000 on a $16,200 roof.
- Add avoided costs + savings: You're net positive by year 5.

**Scenario B — Sell in 10 years:**
- Full ROI calculation returns 166%. You made money.

**Scenario C — Stay 15+ years:**
- The roof pays for itself by year 6 through avoided damage and savings alone. Years 7–15 are pure return.

There is no scenario where a properly timed roof replacement loses money for an Oregon homeowner who holds for more than 5 years. The compounding cost of deferral — structural damage, energy waste, insurance surcharges — makes the "wait and see" strategy the most expensive option.

## The Comparison to Alternatives

"What if I just patch it?" Fair question.

Spot repairs on an aging Oregon roof run $350–$1,200 per incident. The average homeowner with a 20+ year roof needs 2–3 repairs per year. That's $700–$3,600 annually — and each repair is a band-aid on a system that's already past its design life.

After 3 years of patching:
- You've spent $2,100–$10,800
- Your roof's condition has continued to degrade
- Your insurance carrier may have added a surcharge or refused renewal
- Your sheathing has taken additional moisture damage with every leak-between-patches
- Your resale value reflects a 20+ year roof, not a maintained one

The patching path costs 40–65% of a full replacement while delivering 0% of the resale recovery and none of the structural, energy, or insurance benefits.

## The Decision Framework

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **How long will I own this house?** If more than 5 years, replacement ROI is positive.
2. **What's my roof's current age vs. expected life in Oregon conditions?** Subtract 20–30% from the manufacturer's warranty for PNW biological and moisture loading.
3. **Am I paying for emergency repairs more than once per year?** If yes, you've already entered the deferral penalty zone.

Roofing services in Oregon aren't cheap. But they're cheaper than the alternative — which is paying for the same damage twice, once through the repair and once through the equity you forfeit at resale.
