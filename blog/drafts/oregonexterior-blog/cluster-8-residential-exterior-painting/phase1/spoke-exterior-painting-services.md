# 5 Warning Signs Your Oregon Home Needs Repainting Now

Five visible warning signs tell you your Oregon siding's paint has already failed.

Not "might fail soon." Failed. Past tense.

Water is migrating behind the paint film. The substrate is swelling. The bond is breaking.

And waiting another year turns an $8K paint job into a $30K siding replacement.

You can spot all five from the ground. No ladder required. This is your field guide to paint failure in Oregon's 150-day wet season.

## Warning Sign 1: Chalking (The Binder is Breaking Down)

**What you see:**  
Rub your hand on the siding. It leaves a powdery, dusty residue on your palm. The color looks faded or washed-out, especially on south and west-facing walls.

**What's happening:**  
UV radiation and moisture are breaking down the acrylic polymer binder. The pigment particles are no longer held in suspension. They're powdering off.

This is controlled degradation by design — premium paints are formulated to chalk slowly over 10–15 years, self-cleaning as they age. But when chalking accelerates, it means the binder's gone.

**The physics:**  
Paint is a suspension of pigment in a polymer binder. The binder holds the pigment to the substrate. When UV and moisture break the polymer chains, the pigment loses its anchor. What's left is dust.

In Oregon, the combination of high UV (especially at elevation and on south exposures) and constant moisture accelerates this breakdown. A paint job that would chalk gently over 15 years in Arizona chalks aggressively in 8–10 years in Portland.

**How much time you have:**  
1–2 years before the paint film delaminates. Chalking is the early warning. Peeling comes next.

**What happens if you wait:**  
The pigment layer washes off. The substrate is exposed. Wood absorbs moisture. Rot begins. You're replacing boards, not repainting.

**Portland-specific context:**  
Homes in Laurelhurst, Irvington, and Eastmoreland under mature Douglas fir see less UV but higher humidity. Chalking happens slower, but when it starts, moisture penetration happens faster because the canopy holds dew longer.

## Warning Sign 2: Alligatoring (The Topcoat and Primer are Fighting)

**What you see:**  
The paint surface shows a cracked, reptile-scale pattern. The cracks form a grid — sometimes small (1/4 inch), sometimes wide (1 inch+). The pattern is uniform across large sections of wall.

**What's happening:**  
The topcoat and primer (or topcoat and substrate) are expanding and contracting at different rates. This is cohesion failure — the layers aren't moving together.

Common causes:
- **Topcoat applied over incompatible primer** (water-based over oil-based without proper bonding primer)
- **Second coat applied before first coat fully cured** (rushing the schedule in Oregon's damp climate extends cure time)
- **Paint applied over dirty or contaminated surface** (chalk, mildew, or dirt prevents proper adhesion)

**The physics:**  
Paint expands and contracts with temperature and moisture. In Oregon, a 50-degree diurnal temperature swing (40°F at night, 90°F midday in summer) creates constant thermal cycling.

If the topcoat's expansion coefficient doesn't match the primer or substrate, the layers crack. Water enters the cracks. The substrate swells. The cracks widen.

**How much time you have:**  
6 months to 1 year before moisture migration causes substrate damage. Alligatoring means water is already entering the paint film.

**What happens if you wait:**  
Water migrates through the cracks, reaches the substrate, and gets trapped. Wood swells. The entire paint system delaminates in sheets. You're stripping to bare wood and starting over.

**Code reference:**  
The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC) now requires surface preparation testing before recoating alligatored surfaces. Any substrate moisture above 19% voids the repaint warranty.

## Warning Sign 3: Blistering (Moisture is Trapped Beneath the Film)

**What you see:**  
Paint bubbles or blisters, ranging from pinhead-size to quarter-size. Pop one open — if the substrate beneath is wet or discolored, it's moisture blistering. If it's dry, it's solvent blistering (less common).

**What's happening:**  
Water vapor is trapped beneath the paint film. On hot days, the vapor expands and pushes the paint away from the substrate. The bond breaks. The blister forms.

This happens when:
- **Paint applied over wet substrate** (moisture content above 19%)
- **No vapor barrier behind siding** (common in pre-1990s Oregon construction)
- **Interior moisture migrating outward** (bathroom/kitchen humidity escaping through walls)
- **Failed caulking or flashing** (water entering wall assembly from outside)

**The physics:**  
Paint is semi-permeable. Water vapor can migrate through it, but liquid water can't. When vapor gets trapped between the substrate and the paint film, it turns to steam on hot days (vapor pressure increases with temperature). The steam pushes outward. The weakest point — the paint-to-substrate bond — fails first.

In Oregon, north-facing walls with poor solar exposure stay damp longer. Vapor trapped in the substrate can't dry outward (because the exterior stays cool and humid) and can't dry inward (because the paint film blocks it). Blisters form.

**How much time you have:**  
3–6 months before the blistered area delaminates entirely. Once blistering starts, it spreads.

**What happens if you wait:**  
The blistered paint peels off. The substrate beneath is exposed and saturated. Rot begins within one wet season. For wood siding, you're replacing boards. For fiber cement, you're dealing with efflorescence and bond failure.

**Soil-specific context:**  
Portland Hills Silt over Missoula Flood deposits and Tualatin Valley silty clay loam (Beaverton, Tigard) both hold moisture at grade. Homes on these soils see higher foundation and lower-course moisture. Blistering appears first on the bottom 3–4 feet of siding.

## Warning Sign 4: Peeling (The Bond Has Failed)

**What you see:**  
Paint lifting off in sheets, chips, or curls. Sometimes it peels down to bare wood. Sometimes it peels down to an older paint layer.

**What's happening:**  
Adhesion failure. The paint has lost its bond to the substrate.

Common causes:
- **Substrate wasn't clean, dry, or properly primed** at application
- **Moisture entered from behind** (failed flashing, no drainage plane, rising damp from foundation)
- **Incompatible coatings** (latex over oil without bonding primer, or vice versa)
- **Paint applied in wrong conditions** (too cold, too hot, too humid, or over morning dew)

**The physics:**  
Paint adheres through mechanical bonding (it grips the substrate's texture) and chemical bonding (the binder cross-links with the substrate surface). If the substrate is dirty, wet, or contaminated, neither bond forms.

Oregon's 150-day wet season creates two failure modes:

1. **Painters rush the schedule** — They spray in October when substrate moisture is 22% (above the 19% code limit). The paint looks fine for 6 months. Then it peels in sheets.

2. **Moisture enters from behind** — Homes without drainage planes or rainscreen wall assemblies (common in pre-2000 construction) allow water to migrate through the wall cavity. The moisture hits the substrate from behind. The paint blisters, then peels.

**How much time you have:**  
You're past the window. Peeling is substrate exposure. Every day you wait is another day of moisture absorption and rot progression.

**What happens if you wait:**  
Wood rots. Fiber cement delaminates. A $7,500 repaint quote becomes a $20,000–$30,000 siding replacement. We've documented this progression in Lake Oswego and West Linn, where basalt bedrock and perched water tables create drainage problems that telegraph into siding moisture.

**Code reference:**  
The 2026 ORSC requires moisture testing and documentation before repainting over peeling surfaces. If substrate moisture is above 19%, the moisture source must be identified and repaired before recoating.

## Warning Sign 5: Mildew Staining (The Paint's Permeability Has Failed)

**What you see:**  
Black, green, or dark gray streaks on north-facing walls. The staining is usually vertical (following water runoff patterns) or concentrated under eaves and roof overhangs. Scrubbing with water removes some of it, but it returns within weeks.

**What's happening:**  
The paint film's permeability has degraded. Water sits on the surface long enough for *Gloeocapsa magma* (black algae), *Trentepohlia* (green algae), or mildew to colonize.

Healthy paint sheds water. Failed paint holds it.

**The physics:**  
Premium acrylic paints are formulated with biocides (typically zinc pyrithione or copper compounds) that inhibit biological growth. Over 8–12 years, UV and moisture degrade these biocides. The paint loses its resistance.

In Oregon, the combination of high humidity, moderate temperatures (50–70°F most of the year), and dense tree canopy creates ideal conditions for biological growth. Homes in Laurelhurst, Irvington, and Alberta Arts District near mature Douglas fir see mildew staining 2–3 years before homes on open southwest slopes.

**How much time you have:**  
1–2 years before the biological growth penetrates the paint film and reaches the substrate. Once mildew roots into wood, you're dealing with surface rot.

**What happens if you wait:**  
Mildew and algae produce organic acids that break down wood lignin. The surface softens. Screwdrivers sink in. What started as cosmetic staining becomes structural rot.

For fiber cement, biological growth doesn't rot the substrate, but it does accelerate paint degradation. The staining spreads. The paint chalks faster. You're repainting 3–5 years early.

**Tree-specific context:**  
Douglas fir, big leaf maple, and western red cedar canopy in Portland, Beaverton, and Gresham creates 40% more shade and 60% higher humidity than open areas. Mildew staining appears 2–4 years sooner under dense canopy. The 2026 ORSC references tree canopy density as a factor in moisture-management planning.

## The Screwdriver Test: How to Check for Substrate Damage

If you see 2+ of the above warning signs, test the substrate before you commit to a repaint.

**The test:**  
Take a flathead screwdriver. Press the tip into the siding at a 45-degree angle. Apply moderate pressure (not hammering, just leaning).

**If it bounces off:** Substrate is solid. You're repainting.

**If it sinks 1/8 inch or more:** Substrate is softening. You're replacing boards.

**If it punches through:** Substrate is rotted. You're replacing siding.

Do this test at:
- The bottom 2 feet of siding (splash zone, rising damp)
- Under windows (failed flashing common)
- Near downspouts and gutter terminations (overflow/drainage issues)
- On north-facing walls (longest moisture exposure)

If your screwdriver sinks, the paint problem is cosmetic. The substrate problem is structural. Budget accordingly.
