# The Screwdriver Test: How to Check Your Oregon Siding for Hidden Rot

Paint peels. Homeowners repaint. The paint peels again.

This cycle repeats across Portland every 3–5 years, and most of the time the problem isn't the paint. The problem is what's happening behind it. Peeling exterior painting in Portland is almost always a moisture symptom, not a coating failure. And if you repaint over rot, you've just spent $8,000–$15,000 to hide a $25,000 structural problem.

The screwdriver test takes 10 seconds. It tells you whether your siding issue is cosmetic or structural. This guide walks you through the full siding inspection — step by step, with pass/fail criteria at each point — so you know exactly what you're dealing with before you spend a dollar.

## What You Need

- A flat-blade screwdriver (standard size, not a precision screwdriver)
- Your phone for photos
- A tape measure
- A flashlight (for checking under trim and behind downspouts)
- 30–45 minutes for a thorough inspection

## Before You Start: Why Portland Siding Rots Faster

Portland's climate is a rot accelerator. Here's the physics:

Wood reaches its fiber saturation point at roughly 28% moisture content. Above that threshold, fungal decay organisms colonize — brown rot, white rot, soft rot. Below it, they go dormant but don't die. Portland's pattern of 155 rain days followed by incomplete drying keeps siding wood cycling through the decay-active moisture range for 6–8 months per year.

The Hawthorne neighborhood illustrates this perfectly. Homes along Hawthorne Boulevard and up the slopes toward Mt. Tabor Park are predominantly 1910–1940 construction with original old-growth cedar or fir siding. That old-growth material is dense and naturally rot-resistant — but it's 80–110 years old. The extractives (natural preservatives in the wood) are long depleted. What's left is cellulose and lignin held together by habit and paint.

Portland's soil compounds the problem. Portland Hills Silt — the fine-grained loess blanketing the west side — holds moisture at the surface. Missoula Flood deposits under the east side (including Hawthorne) drain faster but still support enough ground moisture to wick into any siding within 6 inches of grade. The 2026 ORSC mandates a minimum 6-inch clearance between siding and soil for exactly this reason.

And then there's the canopy. Douglas fir, big leaf maple, and western red cedar shade siding surfaces, preventing UV drying while dropping organic debris that holds moisture against the wall. Under Portland's Tree Code (Title 11.50), you can't remove most of these trees. You have to manage the moisture they create.

## The Inspection: Step by Step

Work clockwise around your house. Start at the front door. Inspect every wall surface, every trim board, every joint. Photograph anything that fails.

### Step 1: The Visual Sweep (5 minutes)

Walk each wall and look for these indicators:

- **Peeling or bubbling paint.** Paint peels from the substrate side when moisture pushes outward. If paint is peeling at the bottom of a wall section, moisture is wicking up from below. If it's peeling around a window, water is entering the frame joint.
- **Dark staining.** Gray or black discoloration on wood siding indicates sustained moisture. This isn't dirt — it's the early visible stage of fungal colonization.
- **Warping or cupping.** Horizontal lap siding should lie flat. Boards that cup (edges curling away from the wall) are absorbing moisture unevenly — wet on the back face, drier on the painted front face.
- **Gaps at joints.** Where siding boards butt together, where siding meets trim, where trim meets window frames. Any gap wider than 1/16 inch is a water entry point. In Portland's rain exposure, a 1/8-inch gap admits enough water to saturate the sheathing behind it in a single storm.

**Pass:** Uniform paint, flat siding, tight joints.
**Fail:** Any peeling, staining, warping, or gaps wider than 1/16 inch. Mark these locations for the screwdriver test.

### Step 2: The Screwdriver Test — Bottom Edges (10 minutes)

This is the core diagnostic. You're checking the siding and trim at every location where moisture accumulates.

**Start at the bottom.** The lowest course of siding and the bottom edges of all trim boards are the first to rot because they're closest to grade splash-back and gravity-driven moisture.

**The technique:**
1. Place the flat blade of the screwdriver against the wood surface at a 45-degree angle.
2. Push firmly with moderate hand pressure. Not hammering — just a steady push.
3. Assess the resistance.

**Pass:** The screwdriver dents the surface slightly but does not penetrate. The wood feels solid and resists the blade. This is sound wood with intact cellular structure.

**Fail:** The screwdriver sinks in. Any penetration beyond a surface scratch — even 1/8 inch — means the wood fibers have lost structural integrity. This is decay. The wood moisture content has been above 28% long enough for fungal colonization to break down the cellulose.

**Critical fail:** The screwdriver pushes through with little resistance, or the wood crumbles. This is advanced decay. The damage extends well beyond the visible surface. The sheathing and possibly the framing behind this point are compromised.

**Test these locations specifically:**

1. Bottom edge of the lowest siding course, every 4 feet around the house
2. Bottom edge of all corner trim boards
3. Bottom edge of all window and door casing trim
4. Any location where paint is peeling or staining is visible
5. Both sides of every downspout where it contacts the wall
6. Any siding within 12 inches of a plumbing or electrical penetration

### Step 3: The Screwdriver Test — Window and Door Frames (10 minutes)

Window and door frames are the highest-failure-rate locations on any Oregon home. Water runs down the wall, hits the horizontal frame elements, and pools if the sill slope or flashing is inadequate.

**Test every window sill.** Press the screwdriver into the sill at the corners and at the center. Sills rot from the corners inward because that's where water collects.

**Test the bottom corners of every door casing.** Door casings sit on or near the threshold. Splash-back from the entry surface (porch, deck, walkway) drives moisture into the end grain. End grain absorbs water 10–15 times faster than face grain.

**Check the window sill slope.** Place your tape measure or a level on the sill. It should slope away from the glass at a minimum pitch of 5 degrees (roughly 1/4 inch per 3 inches of depth). A flat sill ponds water. An inward-sloping sill directs water into the wall. Either condition guarantees rot within 3–5 years in Portland's climate.

**Pass:** Solid wood at all corners and centers. Positive outward slope on all sills.
**Fail:** Any softness at window sill corners, door casing bases, or flat/reverse-sloped sills.

### Step 4: The Flashing Check (5 minutes)

Flashing is the metal or membrane layer that bridges joints between different materials or building planes. Proper flashing directs water outward. Missing or failed flashing lets water behind the siding.

You can't see most flashing because it's installed behind the siding. But you can check for its presence at key locations:

1. **Roof-to-wall junctions.** Where a roof slope meets a vertical wall, step flashing should be visible as small metal tabs alternating with each shingle course. If you see caulk instead of metal, the flashing is missing or was never installed.
2. **Kickout flashing.** Where a roof-to-wall junction terminates at a gutter, a kickout flashing piece should direct water into the gutter. Missing kickout flashing allows water to run behind the siding. This is the number one cause of concealed wall rot in Portland. One missing kickout can destroy an entire wall cavity over 2–3 years.
3. **Window head flashing.** Above each window, there should be a Z-shaped metal flashing (head flashing) that directs water over the window frame. It's often hidden behind the trim, but you can sometimes see its bottom edge. If water staining is concentrated above a window, the head flashing is likely failed or absent.

**Pass:** Metal visible at all roof-to-wall junctions and kickout locations. No staining patterns suggesting flashing failure above windows.
**Fail:** Caulk substituting for metal flashing, missing kickout flashing, or staining above windows.

### Step 5: Ground Clearance Measurement (5 minutes)

The 2026 ORSC requires a minimum 6-inch clearance between the bottom of siding and finished grade. This is non-negotiable. Siding in contact with soil wicks moisture directly into the wall assembly through capillary action.

**Measure at every corner and at the midpoint of every wall.** Soil grade changes around a house, especially in Portland's hilly neighborhoods like Hawthorne where lots slope toward Mt. Tabor or down toward the river.

Common violations:
- **Landscaping buildup.** Mulch, soil, and bark dust get added over the years and creep up the foundation line. Pull mulch away from siding.
- **Porch or deck attachment.** Where a deck or porch ledger board attaches to the house, the flashing detail and clearance are critical. Many Portland homes have decks with improper ledger flashing that traps moisture against the band joist.
- **Settled grade.** On Portland Hills Silt, the soil next to the foundation can settle and create reverse grade (sloping toward the house). This concentrates surface water at the foundation-to-siding junction.

**Pass:** 6 inches or more of clearance between siding and grade at all points. No soil, mulch, or debris contacting the siding.
**Fail:** Any point where clearance is less than 6 inches or where organic material contacts the siding.

### Step 6: The Probe Map (5 minutes)

Take all your photos and notes from Steps 1–5 and create a simple sketch or annotated photo set showing:

- Every location where the screwdriver test failed
- Every location where flashing is missing or suspect
- Every clearance violation
- Every visual indicator (peeling, staining, warping)

This map tells you three things:
1. **Scope.** How much of the exterior is affected?
2. **Pattern.** Are failures concentrated on one side (exposure issue) or scattered (systemic issue)?
3. **Priority.** Structural failures (screwdriver fails, missing flashing) come before cosmetic failures (peeling paint on sound wood).

## Interpreting Your Results

### Scenario A: Cosmetic Only

Sound wood everywhere. Paint peeling but screwdriver test passes. Joints tight. Flashing present.

**Action:** Clean, prep, and repaint. Exterior painting in Portland is effective when the substrate is sound. Use a high-quality acrylic latex with a primer coat on any bare wood. This is a $6,000–$12,000 job for a typical Portland home, and it should last 7–10 years with proper prep.

### Scenario B: Isolated Rot

Screwdriver failures in 1–3 locations, typically window sills, door casings, or the lowest siding course. The rest of the siding is sound.

**Action:** Targeted repair before painting. Remove the decayed material back to sound wood. Check the sheathing behind. If the sheathing is sound, install new trim or siding with proper flashing and sealant. Then paint. Adding painting over isolated rot without repair is wasted money — the rot will push through the new paint within 2 years.

### Scenario C: Systemic Rot

Multiple screwdriver failures across multiple walls. Flashing deficiencies. Ground clearance violations.

**Action:** This is beyond a paint job. You need a siding contractor to evaluate the wall assembly — sheathing, framing, insulation, vapor barrier. In Hawthorne-area homes built before 1950, systemic rot often traces back to a combination of original construction without house wrap or drainage plane, decades of deferred maintenance, and Portland's unrelenting moisture load. Budget $15,000–$40,000 depending on scope. Address flashing and drainage first, then siding, then paint.

### Scenario D: Paint Peeling but No Visible Cause

Sound wood, good flashing, adequate clearance — but paint keeps peeling.

**Action:** The moisture is likely coming from inside the wall. Interior humidity migrating through the wall assembly condenses on the back side of the siding and pushes paint off. This is common in Portland homes with poor vapor management — no interior vapor retarder, or a vapor retarder on the wrong side of the insulation. A professional moisture assessment is warranted.

## The Mt. Tabor Factor

Homes on the slopes around Mt. Tabor Park face amplified versions of every problem described above. The park's 196 acres of Douglas fir, western red cedar, and big leaf maple create a microclimate that extends into the surrounding blocks. Shade is persistent. Air circulation is limited. Moss and algae colonize siding surfaces directly — not just roofs.

The east-facing slopes get full morning sun and afternoon shade from the canopy, creating a daily wet-dry cycle that accelerates paint failure. The west-facing slopes stay damp longer and see more biological growth. If your home borders Mt. Tabor Park, increase your inspection frequency to twice per year — spring and fall.

The geology under Hawthorne is Missoula Flood deposits overlying Boring Lava basalt. The flood deposits are gravelly and drain well compared to the Portland Hills Silt on the west side. But the basalt-derived clay subsoil has high shrink-swell potential, and seasonal ground movement stresses foundation walls and the siding attachments above them. Cracked caulk joints in Hawthorne are often settlement-driven, not just age-driven.
