# Professional vs. DIY: When to Call an Oregon Exterior Expert

Here's the rule. It's simple and it's absolute:

If the fix is above the gutter line, call a pro. If the fix is behind the wall, call a pro. Everything else is fair game.

That rule has saved Oregon homeowners from two kinds of expensive mistakes: paying a contractor $800 for something they could have done in 20 minutes, and DIY-ing a repair that turned a $2,000 problem into a $12,000 problem. Both happen constantly. This guide gives you the decision tree — clear boundaries, no ambiguity — so you spend money only where it matters.

## The Decision Tree

Every exterior problem falls into one of four categories. Work through these in order.

### Category 1: DIY — Do It This Weekend

These are maintenance tasks that require no specialized skills, no specialized tools, and no risk of making the problem worse. If you own a ladder, a caulk gun, and a garden hose, you can handle all of these.

**Gutter cleaning and maintenance**

Clogged gutters are the number one deferred maintenance item on Portland homes. The Douglas fir, big leaf maple, and western red cedar canopy across neighborhoods like Multnomah Village drops needles, leaves, and seed pods into gutter runs from September through May.

- Clean gutters twice per year minimum (November and March)
- Check downspout discharge — water should exit at least 4 feet from the foundation
- Re-secure loose gutter spikes or hangers with a hammer or drill
- Seal small gutter joint leaks with gutter sealant

**Time:** 1–2 hours. **Cost:** $0–$30 in sealant. **Risk if you skip it:** Foundation settlement on Portland Hills Silt, fascia rot, crawlspace flooding.

**Caulk replacement**

Every joint where siding meets trim, where trim meets window frames, and where penetrations pass through the wall needs flexible sealant. In Portland's climate, exterior caulk lasts 5–7 years before UV and thermal cycling degrades it.

- Remove old caulk with a utility knife and caulk removal tool
- Clean the joint surfaces
- Apply a high-quality polyurethane or silicone sealant rated for exterior use
- Tool the bead smooth. Caulk should bond to both surfaces and fill the joint completely.

**Time:** 2–4 hours for a full house. **Cost:** $30–$60 in caulk. **Risk if you skip it:** Water intrusion through every failed joint. In Portland's rain exposure, one season of failed caulk equals one season of wall cavity wetting.

**Vegetation clearance**

Shrubs, vines, and ground cover touching your foundation or siding hold moisture against the structure and provide pathways for insects.

- Maintain 12 inches minimum between vegetation and siding
- Pull mulch or bark dust back from the foundation line — maintain 6 inches of visible foundation per 2026 ORSC
- Trim tree branches that overhang the roof by less than 10 feet

**Time:** 1–3 hours. **Cost:** $0. **Risk if you skip it:** Accelerated biological growth, insect intrusion, concealed moisture damage.

**Debris removal from roof valleys**

Using a roof rake (a long-handled tool operated from the ground), clear accumulated needles and leaves from roof valleys. Don't climb the roof. Don't use a pressure washer.

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Cost:** $30–$50 for a roof rake. **Risk if you skip it:** Valley debris dams redirect water under shingle lapping edges. Valley leaks are expensive — $1,500–$4,000 to repair because they require shingle removal and re-flashing.

### Category 2: DIY With Caution — Know Your Limits

These tasks are technically within DIY capability but carry risk of causing damage if done incorrectly. Proceed only if you're confident in the technique.

**Minor paint touch-up on sound wood**

If the screwdriver test confirms sound substrate, you can scrape, sand, prime, and repaint limited areas. Key points:

- Scrape all loose paint to a firm edge. Don't feather over soft paint — it'll peel again.
- Sand to a smooth transition between bare wood and adhered paint.
- Apply an oil-based or shellac-based primer to bare wood. Latex primer on bare wood in Portland's moisture conditions is a failure waiting to happen.
- Top-coat with quality acrylic latex.

**Risk if done wrong:** Painting over unsound wood or skipping primer leads to another peel cycle. You've wasted time and money and the rot underneath is now six months worse.

**Gutter guard installation**

Micro-mesh or reverse-curve gutter guards reduce cleaning frequency. They don't eliminate cleaning — nothing does under Portland's canopy — but they change the task from scooping debris out of the trough to brushing it off the guard surface.

- Ensure the guard sits flush with the shingle edge. Guards that lift the shingle edge break the drainage seal and can void the shingle warranty.
- Fastening patterns matter. Screw-down guards attached to the fascia add wind uplift resistance. Clip-on guards dislodge in the east wind events Portland sees every fall.

**Risk if done wrong:** Improperly installed guards can dam water behind the gutter, redirect water behind the fascia, or void your roof warranty.

**Downspout extensions and splash blocks**

Extending downspout discharge away from the foundation is critical on Portland soils. Portland Hills Silt compresses when saturated. Boring Lava clay swells. Either way, concentrated water at your foundation is structural damage over time.

- Extend downspouts to discharge at least 4 feet from the foundation
- Better: connect to a buried drain line that runs to daylight or a dry well
- Grade the soil away from the house at all discharge points

**Risk if done wrong:** Minimal. The main risk is running a buried drain line with insufficient slope, which creates a clog point. Maintain 1/8-inch per foot minimum slope.

### Category 3: Call a Pro — The Gutter Line Rule

The gutter line is the dividing line between homeowner maintenance and professional territory. Above it, the risk profile changes fundamentally.

**Any roof repair**

This includes:
- Shingle replacement (even one shingle)
- Valley re-flashing
- Ridge cap repair or replacement
- Pipe boot replacement
- Chimney flashing repair
- Skylight flashing repair

**Why you shouldn't DIY this:** Roofing services in Oregon require understanding fastening patterns, shingle lapping geometry, flashing integration, and ice-and-water shield placement. A shingle installed with the wrong nail placement (too high, too low, or too few nails) won't fail immediately. It'll fail during the next windstorm when the shingle tabs lift and water enters. The 2026 ORSC specifies fastening requirements for the Willamette Valley wind zone. Get them wrong and you void the shingle warranty and create a code compliance issue.

**What it costs:** $300–$800 for a minor repair (a few shingles, a pipe boot). $1,500–$4,000 for valley or chimney re-flashing. $8,000–$25,000+ for significant roof work. Oregon Exterior Experts provides free roof inspections to assess scope before recommending work.

**Any work requiring removal of siding**

If the screwdriver test found rot, the repair requires removing siding to evaluate and replace damaged material. This means:
- Removing siding without damaging adjacent courses
- Evaluating sheathing and framing behind the siding
- Replacing damaged sheathing or framing members
- Installing proper house wrap or drainage plane integration
- Re-installing siding with correct lapping, clearances, and fastening

**Why you shouldn't DIY this:** The wall assembly behind your siding is a system — structure, insulation, vapor management, drainage plane, cladding. Repairing one layer incorrectly compromises the others. A siding repair without proper house wrap integration creates a concealed moisture trap worse than the original damage.

**What it costs:** $500–$2,000 for isolated trim or siding replacement. $5,000–$15,000 for a wall section. $15,000–$40,000 for full siding replacement on a Portland home.

**Flashing installation or repair**

Step flashing, kickout flashing, head flashing, counter-flashing — these are the unsung critical details that keep water out of your wall cavities. Missing or incorrect flashing is the most common cause of catastrophic concealed moisture damage in Oregon homes.

**Why you shouldn't DIY this:** Flashing requires integration with the roof system above and the drainage plane below. A kickout flashing installed at the wrong angle diverts water but doesn't fully clear the gutter. Counter-flashing set into mortar joints requires proper reglet cutting and sealant. Step flashing must interweave with shingle courses — removing and replacing shingles is roof work (see above).

**What it costs:** $200–$600 per flashing location for straightforward repairs. $1,000–$3,000 if siding removal is required for access.

### Category 4: Call a Pro Immediately — Active Damage

These conditions indicate damage that is worsening right now. Every day of delay increases the repair scope and cost.

**Water staining on interior ceilings or walls**

Interior staining means the exterior envelope has been breached and water is reaching the framing and interior finishes. The leak source may not be directly above the stain — water travels along framing members and can appear feet or even rooms away from the entry point.

**Action:** Call a roofing services professional. Do not try to trace the leak from outside — interior water tracing requires systematic testing, often with a hose simulating rain at various points while someone monitors inside. Roof leak tracing is a diagnostic skill, not a repair task.

**Soft or spongy subfloor near exterior walls**

This typically indicates foundation-level water intrusion that has wicked into the floor framing. Common in Portland crawlspace homes where gutter discharge or grade issues are directing water against the foundation.

**Action:** Call a foundation or crawlspace specialist. The floor framing may need evaluation and the moisture source must be identified and corrected at the exterior.

**Horizontal or stair-step cracks in the foundation**

Vertical hairline cracks are normal concrete shrinkage. Horizontal cracks mean lateral soil pressure is bowing the foundation wall. Stair-step cracks in block foundations indicate differential settlement. On Portland Hills Silt and Missoula Flood deposits, these patterns correlate with seasonal moisture loading in the soil.

**Action:** Call a structural engineer. Not a contractor — an engineer. You need a diagnosis before you need a repair. Budget $500–$1,000 for a structural evaluation. The repair, if needed, can range from $5,000 for carbon fiber reinforcement to $30,000+ for underpinning.

**Active mold visible on exterior or interior surfaces**

Mold on exterior siding is a moisture indicator. Mold on interior surfaces near exterior walls is a building envelope failure. Oregon's climate supports aggressive mold growth — Stachybotrys (black mold) and Aspergillus colonize damp building materials rapidly once the moisture content exceeds 20%.

**Action:** Do not disturb the mold. Call a mold assessment professional. If the affected area exceeds 10 square feet, Oregon Health Authority guidelines recommend professional remediation.

## The Multnomah Village Reality

Walk through Multnomah Village — down Capitol Highway past Riverview Cemetery, along the blocks of 1940s–1960s ranch homes and split-levels — and you'll see why this decision tree matters.

These homes were built with materials and methods that were standard for the era: no house wrap, minimal flashing, cedar or fir siding nailed directly over board sheathing. They've survived 60–80 years because the materials were decent and the original craftsmanship was solid. But they're reaching the end of their unassisted service life.

The Multnomah Village canopy is dense. Western red cedar and big leaf maple crowd the streets. Moss grows on siding, not just roofs. Gutters fill with debris every month from October through April. The soil is Portland Hills Silt — loess that holds moisture and drives seasonal foundation movement.

The homeowners who walk their exterior every season and handle the Category 1 and 2 items themselves keep these homes functional for another generation. The homeowners who defer everything and then call a contractor when the ceiling is staining spend three to five times as much and still end up with a house that needs more work.

## What to Expect When You Call a Pro

If your inspection has identified Category 3 or 4 issues, here's what the professional process looks like:

**Step 1: Assessment.** A qualified contractor walks the exterior with you, reviews your findings, and identifies the full scope. Good contractors explain what they see, show you the problem areas, and provide options. At Oregon Exterior Experts, assessments are free and include a written report.

**Step 2: Scope and estimate.** The contractor defines the work scope, materials, and timeline. Get this in writing. The estimate should itemize: demolition/removal, materials, labor, flashing details, and any code compliance items. In Oregon, contractors must be licensed with the Construction Contractors Board (CCB) and carry liability insurance. Verify both.

**Step 3: The work.** Professional exterior work in Portland is weather-dependent. The dry window (July–September) is optimal for siding and painting. Roofing can be done year-round with appropriate underlayment protection. Expect 1–3 days for targeted repairs, 1–3 weeks for full siding replacement.

**Step 4: Documentation.** Permits, inspection sign-offs, warranty certificates, and before/after photos. This documentation matters for insurance claims, resale, and future maintenance planning.

## The Cost of Waiting: A Simple Model

The cost escalation of deferred exterior maintenance follows a predictable pattern:

$$C_{repair} = C_{base} \times (1 + r)^t$$

Where $C_{base}$ is the repair cost if addressed today, $r$ is the annual damage escalation rate (typically 0.3–0.5 for active water intrusion in Portland's climate), and $t$ is years of deferral. A $2,000 flashing repair deferred for 3 years at a 0.4 escalation rate becomes:

$$C_{repair} = 2000 \times (1.4)^3 = \$5,488$$

That's not a scare number. That's the math. Water damage is exponential because each failure enables the next: failed flashing wets the sheathing, wet sheathing rots the framing, rotted framing shifts the siding, shifted siding opens more joints, more joints admit more water. The feedback loop accelerates.
