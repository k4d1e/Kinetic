## The Physics of Exterior Failure in Oregon

Before the checklist, you need to understand why Oregon eats houses faster than most of the country.

Three forces work simultaneously against your exterior envelope:

### 1. Moisture Loading

Portland's rain isn't the problem. Portland's rain *duration* is the problem. The city averages 155 days of measurable precipitation per year. That's 155 wetting cycles with incomplete drying between them. Materials that perform fine in climates with distinct wet-dry cycles — fiber cement, composite shingles, wood trim — stay saturated here for weeks at a time.

The moisture content of wood siding in a properly detailed wall assembly should stay below 19%. In Portland's west hills, field measurements routinely show 25–30% in walls with even minor flashing defects. At 28% moisture content, fungal decay initiates. The timeline from first colonization to structural compromise is 18–36 months.

### 2. Biological Load

The urban canopy in neighborhoods like Irvington near Grant Park creates a microclimate on your roof and siding. Douglas fir needles acidify surface runoff. Big leaf maple leaves mat in valleys and behind trim boards. Western red cedar pollen and debris feed moss, algae, and lichen colonies.

Moss doesn't just look bad. It holds water against surfaces that were designed to shed it. A moss mat on a roof slope retains 15–20 times its dry weight in water. That's dead load the framing wasn't engineered for, sitting on a shingle surface that was designed to drain in seconds.

### 3. Soil-Driven Settlement

Portland Hills Silt is a wind-deposited loess — fine-grained, poorly consolidated, and moisture-sensitive. When it gets wet, it compresses. When it dries, it shrinks. This seasonal volume change drives foundation micro-movement that propagates up through the framing and manifests as cracked siding joints, separated trim, and gaps at window and door openings.

The Boring Lava basalt in Portland's east hills is more stable, but homes there face a different problem: basalt-derived clay soils with high shrink-swell potential that stresses foundation walls laterally.

Either way, the ground is moving. Your exterior has to accommodate that movement or crack.

## The 5-Minute Exterior Walk: Safety-First Checklist

Grab your phone for photos. Start at your front door and walk clockwise. You're looking at five systems. Each one protects the one behind it.

### System 1: Roof Plane (From the Ground)

You don't need a ladder — a ground-level roof audit built on [roof cleaning services](/blog/roof-cleaning-services) knowledge spots the same damage patterns a pro sees from the top.

**Check these from the ground with binoculars or your phone camera zoomed in:**

- [ ] **Shingle alignment.** Shingles should form straight horizontal lines. Wavy lines mean the underlayment or deck sheathing is failing. Wavy = moisture damage = structural.
- [ ] **Granule coverage.** Bare dark patches on composite shingles mean granule loss. Check your gutter discharge — if you see granule sand accumulating, the shingles are past half-life.
- [ ] **Moss and debris.** Any visible moss thicker than surface discoloration is holding water against your shingles. Debris dams in valleys redirect water under shingle lapping edges.
- [ ] **Ridge cap integrity.** The ridge cap (the shingles at the peak) should sit flat and uniform. Lifted, cracked, or missing caps expose the ridge vent and deck to direct water entry.
- [ ] **Flashing visibility.** You should see metal flashing at every roof-to-wall junction, chimney, skylight, and plumbing vent. If you see caulk instead of metal, that's a failure waiting to happen.

**Pass/fail:** If you see wavy shingle lines, large bare patches, or missing flashing — that's not a maintenance item. That's a repair. Flag it.

### System 2: Gutters and Drainage

Gutters aren't decorative. They're the primary drainage system keeping water away from your foundation. In Portland's soil conditions — Portland Hills Silt that compresses when wet — a failed gutter system doesn't just mean water staining. It means foundation settlement.

- [ ] **Gutter sag.** Sight along the gutter run. It should slope consistently toward the downspout. Sags or flat spots mean standing water, which means weight, which means more sag. It's a progressive failure.
- [ ] **Overflow staining.** Dark vertical streaks below the gutter lip mean water is overtopping. Cause: clogs, undersized gutters, or insufficient slope.
- [ ] **Downspout discharge.** Every downspout should discharge into a drain pipe, splash block, or extension that moves water at least 4 feet from the foundation. Water pooling at the foundation is the fastest path to a crawlspace problem.
- [ ] **Gutter-to-fascia connection.** Pull gently on the gutter edge. Movement means the fascia board behind it is softening. Soft fascia = hidden rot behind the gutter = sub-fascia and rafter tail damage.

**Pass/fail:** Standing water in gutters, visible overflow staining, or any discharge within 2 feet of the foundation — these are active damage conditions. Don't wait on these.

### System 3: Siding and Cladding

The screwdriver test takes 10 seconds — if it sinks into your Portland siding, the [exterior painting](/blog/exterior-painting-portland) problem you see is actually structural rot underneath.

- [ ] **Caulk joints.** Check every joint where siding meets trim, window frames, and door frames. Cracked, pulled, or missing caulk is an open path for water. In Portland's rain exposure, failed caulk becomes interior moisture within one season.
- [ ] **Paint or stain condition.** Peeling paint is a moisture indicator, not just a cosmetic issue. Paint peels from the back side — moisture is pushing out from inside the wall. Bubbling or alligatoring means the substrate is wet.
- [ ] **Siding lapping.** Horizontal siding (lap siding, clapboard) should overlap consistently. Gaps, warping, or buckling at lapping edges expose the wall sheathing behind.
- [ ] **Ground clearance.** Siding must clear the soil by at least 6 inches per 2026 ORSC. Less than that and you're wicking ground moisture directly into your wall assembly. Check near landscaping beds where soil builds up over time.
- [ ] **Kick-out flashing.** Where a roof slope meets a sidewall, there must be a kick-out flashing directing water into the gutter — not behind the siding. Missing kick-out flashing is the number one cause of concealed wall rot in Oregon. One missing piece can destroy an entire wall cavity.

**Pass/fail:** Soft wood anywhere (use the screwdriver test on trim boards), missing kick-out flashing, or siding-to-soil contact — these are structural, not cosmetic.

### System 4: Windows, Doors, and Penetrations

Every hole in your exterior envelope is a potential failure point. Windows, doors, vents, hose bibs, light fixtures — each one interrupts the drainage plane and relies on flashing and sealant to maintain continuity.

- [ ] **Window sill slope.** Window sills should slope away from the glass. A flat or inward-sloping sill ponds water against the frame-to-wall junction. In wood-framed homes, this destroys the rough sill framing within 3–5 years.
- [ ] **Sealant continuity.** Run your eye around every window and door frame. The sealant should be continuous, flexible, and bonded to both surfaces. Gaps, cracks, or pulled edges mean water is entering.
- [ ] **Weep holes.** Vinyl and aluminum windows have small weep holes at the bottom of the frame. Clogged weep holes trap water inside the frame, which overflows into the wall assembly. Clear them with a toothpick.
- [ ] **Penetration seals.** Every pipe, wire, vent, and fixture that passes through your siding needs a sealed flashing or boot. Check dryer vents, bathroom exhaust vents, and electrical meter boxes.

**Pass/fail:** Any sealant failure around windows or doors in Portland's rain exposure is an active leak path. Prioritize these.

### System 5: Foundation and Grade

The ground level is where everything drains to — or doesn't.

- [ ] **Visible cracks.** Hairline cracks in concrete foundations are normal shrinkage. Cracks wider than 1/4 inch, horizontal cracks, or stair-step cracks in block foundations indicate structural movement. On Portland Hills Silt, this often correlates with seasonal moisture cycles.
- [ ] **Grade slope.** The soil surface should slope away from the foundation at a minimum of 6 inches in the first 10 feet. Negative grade (sloping toward the house) funnels every rainstorm directly into your foundation and crawlspace.
- [ ] **Crawlspace vents.** If your home has a vented crawlspace, check that vents are open and unobstructed. Blocked vents trap moisture below the floor framing. The 2026 ORSC also allows sealed/conditioned crawlspaces — if yours is sealed, the vapor barrier should be visible and intact from the vent openings.
- [ ] **Vegetation clearance.** Shrubs, vines, and ground cover should not contact the foundation or siding. Direct contact holds moisture against the structure and provides insect pathways. Maintain at least 12 inches of clearance.

**Pass/fail:** Horizontal or stair-step cracks, negative grade at the foundation, or standing water near the foundation after rain — these are immediate-action items.

## The Irvington Reality Check

Walk around a typical Irvington home near Grant Park and you'll see why this checklist matters here more than almost anywhere else in Portland.

Irvington homes are predominantly 1910–1930 construction. Craftsman bungalows and Foursquares with original wood siding, single-pane windows, and foundation systems designed for a different era. The neighborhood's mature canopy — towering Douglas firs and big leaf maples lining every block — is beautiful but relentless. Roof surfaces on the north side of NE Knott Street carry visible moss by November. Gutter systems overflow by December. By February, water has been working every failed joint for four solid months.

The soil underneath is classic Portland Hills Silt — loess deposited during the Pleistocene, sitting over Missoula Flood gravels. It holds water well. Too well. Foundation drainage in Irvington homes is often original clay tile — partially collapsed, root-invaded, and functionally useless. When the silt saturates, it compresses under the footings, and the house settles unevenly. That settlement cracks siding joints, opens trim gaps, and breaks sealant lines — which lets in more water. The cycle accelerates.

This is not a reason to panic. It's a reason to walk your exterior once a season and catch the small stuff before it compounds.

## The 2026 Code Context

The 2026 ORSC doesn't mandate that you walk around your house with a checklist. But it does tighten the standards your house must meet.

Roof assemblies must maintain their rated weather resistance. Exterior cladding must maintain a functional drainage plane. Flashing at all roof-to-wall and wall-to-window junctions must meet current installation standards — which means properties sold or refinanced may trigger inspection requirements that flag deferred maintenance.

Portland's Title 24 stormwater overlay means your roof and gutter system must drain to design capacity. Clogged gutters and moss-dammed valleys aren't just maintenance issues — they're stormwater compliance problems.

And Title 11.50 means you can't solve the canopy problem by removing the trees. The city's Tree Code requires permits for most tree removals and mandates canopy preservation in many neighborhoods. Active maintenance of your exterior envelope is, effectively, a regulatory requirement.

## When to DIY and When to Call

The line between DIY and calling [roofing services in Oregon](/blog/roofing-services-oregon) is simple: if the fix is above the gutter line or behind the wall, call a pro.

Here's the decision framework:

**DIY territory:**
- Clearing gutters and downspouts
- Replacing cracked caulk at siding joints and window frames
- Clearing debris from valleys (from the ground with a roof rake)
- Maintaining vegetation clearance
- Monitoring and documenting cracks, stains, and changes

**Pro territory:**
- Any work requiring a ladder above single-story eave height
- Flashing repair or replacement
- Siding repair where rot is present behind the cladding
- Foundation crack evaluation (anything beyond hairline)
- Roof repairs — shingle replacement, valley re-flashing, ridge cap work

The cost of a professional exterior inspection in Portland runs $200–$500. The cost of the damage that inspection catches early runs $5,000–$25,000. The math is straightforward.
