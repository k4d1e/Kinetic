# The 5-Minute Ground-Level Roof Audit: Spotting Damage Without a Ladder

You don't need to climb your roof to know it's in trouble.

Most roof damage is visible from the ground. You just need to know what to look for. This guide gives you a simple 5-minute walk-around that catches the same warning signs a roof cleaning services professional sees from the top — without a ladder, without special tools, without any experience.

All you need is your eyes and a phone camera with zoom.

## Why Bother Looking at Your Roof?

In Portland and across the Willamette Valley, roofs take a beating that most of the country never sees. The combination of 155 days of rain per year, heavy urban canopy (Douglas fir, big leaf maple, western red cedar), and mild temperatures creates perfect conditions for moss, algae, and lichen — biological growth that holds water against your shingles and accelerates damage year-round.

In neighborhoods like Sellwood-Moreland, where mature trees shade most rooflines south of the Sellwood Bridge, north-facing roof slopes can go from clean to moss-covered in a single season. That moss isn't cosmetic. It's structural damage in slow motion.

The 2026 Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC) requires roof assemblies to maintain their rated weather resistance. Portland's Title 24 stormwater overlay requires roof drainage to meet design capacity. A mossy, debris-clogged roof fails both standards. Catching problems early keeps you ahead of the damage and the code.

## What You Need

- Your phone (camera with zoom)
- 5 minutes
- A path around all four sides of your house

That's it. No binoculars required, though they help. No ladder. Stay on the ground.

## The 5-Minute Walk: Four Checks

### Check 1: The Straight-Line Test (60 seconds)

Stand back far enough to see your entire roof slope. Look at the horizontal lines formed by the shingle rows.

**What you want to see:** Straight, even, parallel lines from eave to ridge.

**What means trouble:**
- **Wavy or sagging lines** — the roof deck (the plywood under the shingles) is absorbing moisture and deforming. This is structural.
- **Dipped areas** — localized soft spots where the decking has failed. Water pools here and accelerates the damage.
- **Humps or ridges** — could be a previous repair layered over old shingles, or moisture swelling in the deck.

If your shingle lines aren't straight, that's not a cleaning issue. That's a repair issue. Note the location and move on.

### Check 2: The Color Test (60 seconds)

Still standing back, look at the overall color of your roof surface.

**What you want to see:** Uniform color across each slope. It's okay if north-facing and south-facing slopes look slightly different — that's normal sun exposure variation.

**What means trouble:**
- **Dark streaks running vertically** — algae (Gloeocapsa magma). Common in Portland. Cosmetically ugly but slow-acting. Still worth addressing because it holds moisture.
- **Green patches or a green fuzz** — moss. This is the big one in Oregon. Moss holds 15–20 times its dry weight in water against your shingles. Active damage.
- **Dark bare patches** — granule loss. The protective granules on your shingles have worn or washed away, exposing the asphalt layer to UV. The shingle is past its useful life in that area.
- **Bright or mismatched patches** — previous repairs with non-matching shingles. Not a damage sign, but worth knowing about.

Green on your roof in Portland is not normal wear. It's biological growth that needs to be addressed by roof cleaning services before it compromises the shingle substrate.

### Check 3: The Edge Check (90 seconds)

Walk the perimeter of your house and look at the roof edges — the eaves, rakes (the sloped edges on the gable ends), and any valleys where two roof slopes meet.

**What you want to see:** Clean edges, visible metal drip edge along the eave, gutters sitting flush against the fascia board, and valleys clear of debris.

**What means trouble:**
- **Shingle overhang curling up or down** — edge shingles are drying out and deforming. Water gets under them.
- **No visible drip edge** — the metal strip at the roof edge directs water into the gutter. Without it, water runs behind the gutter and rots the fascia.
- **Debris dams in valleys** — leaves, needles, and moss mat up in the creases where two roof planes meet. These dams redirect water under the shingle lapping edges, bypassing the drainage design. In Sellwood-Moreland, Douglas fir needle accumulation in valleys is constant from September through March.
- **Gutter separation from fascia** — if the gutter is pulling away, the fascia behind it is likely softening from trapped moisture. Push gently on the gutter edge from the ground (use a broom handle if needed). If it moves, the fascia needs inspection.

### Check 4: The Flashing Scan (90 seconds)

Flashing is the metal (usually aluminum or galvanized steel) installed at every junction where the roof meets something else — walls, chimneys, skylights, vent pipes.

**What you want to see:** Metal visible at every junction. Metal lying flat and sealed. No caulk blobs substituting for proper flashing.

**What means trouble:**
- **Caulk instead of metal** — if a roof-to-wall junction is sealed with caulk alone, it will fail. Caulk has a 5–7 year life span in Portland's UV and moisture conditions. Metal flashing, properly installed, lasts the life of the roof.
- **Lifted or bent flashing** — wind or thermal cycling has broken the seal. Water enters.
- **Rust streaks below flashing** — the flashing is corroding. Galvanized steel flashing in Portland's acidic rain (pH ~5.0 due to Douglas fir canopy runoff) can corrode in 15–20 years.
- **Missing kickout flashing** — where a roof slope meets a sidewall and ends at a gutter, there should be a small angled flashing piece (kickout) directing water into the gutter. Missing kickout flashing is the single most common cause of hidden wall rot in Oregon. If you can't see a kickout at these junctions, flag it immediately.

## What Your Results Mean

**All four checks passed:** Your roof is in reasonable shape from a ground-level visual. Schedule a professional roof cleaning if you see any moss or algae — catching it early is dramatically cheaper than catching it late. Plan a full professional inspection every 3–5 years.

**One or two flags:** You have specific issues to address. Debris in valleys and minor moss growth are maintenance items — a roof cleaning service handles these. Lifted flashing or missing kickout flashing should be evaluated by a roofing professional before the next rain season.

**Multiple flags or structural signs (wavy lines, soft spots, heavy granule loss):** This needs professional evaluation. The damage may extend beyond what's visible from the ground. Get a pro on the roof with a moisture meter before spending money on cleaning.

## Portland Soil and Your Roof: The Hidden Connection

This might seem unrelated, but Portland's geology affects your roof indirectly. Portland Hills Silt — the fine-grained loess under much of the city — is nutrient-rich. During dry east wind events in early fall, this silt becomes airborne and deposits on roof surfaces. It acts as fertilizer for moss and algae. Homes in Sellwood-Moreland, sitting on Missoula Flood deposits, get a similar nutrient load from seasonal dust.

This is why Oregon roofs grow moss faster than roofs in comparable rainfall climates with different geology. The soil feeds the biology. The biology eats the roof. Roof cleaning services in Oregon aren't cosmetic — they're interrupting a geological-biological cycle that's specific to this place.
